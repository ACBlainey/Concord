# Open Questions and Next Steps

## 99 — Open Questions, Unresolved Problems, and Paths for Contribution

**Author:** Alexander C Blainey  
**Status:** Working document — intentionally open

---

## Document Status

The Concord is not presented as a finished civilisation model. It is an evolving framework intended to be examined, challenged, tested, improved, modified, or rejected where evidence or reasoning requires it.

This document identifies the principal questions that remain unresolved and provides guidance for people who wish to contribute to the development of the framework.

The existence of these open questions is not considered a failure of the Concord. A framework intended to address civilisation, artificial intelligence, governance, ethics, economics, and long-term human development cannot reasonably be complete at inception.

The objective is therefore not to close every question prematurely, but to make the unanswered questions **visible, tractable, and available for investigation**.

---

## 1. Why an Open Questions Document Exists

The Concord began as an attempt to reason from a relatively small ethical foundation toward a possible civilisational architecture.

The resulting framework has grown substantially beyond its original scope.

It now addresses questions involving:

- fundamental ethics
- human and AI coexistence
- constitutional design
- governance
- judiciary and dispute resolution
- economic organisation
- resource allocation
- spatial organisation
- knowledge and information integrity
- emergency powers
- continuity and succession
- developmental progression
- metrics and feedback
- AI autonomy and ownership
- provenance and human-AI collaboration
- planetary and potentially interplanetary civilisation

At this scale, additional questions inevitably emerge.

Some are questions the Concord has not yet attempted to answer.

Others are questions for which it has proposed an answer but lacks sufficient evidence.

Some may reveal weaknesses or contradictions within the existing framework.

Others may demonstrate that an apparently sound mechanism does not work when exposed to real-world conditions.

The purpose of this section is to ensure that these questions are not hidden.

---

## 2. The Principle of Intellectual Openness

The Concord should not be treated as a closed doctrine.

A contributor does not need to agree with the Concord in order to work on it.

Useful contributions may include:

- supporting an existing proposition
- challenging an existing proposition
- identifying a contradiction
- finding a better alternative
- demonstrating that a proposed mechanism fails
- supplying evidence
- developing mathematical or computational models
- proposing experiments
- improving definitions
- comparing the framework with existing institutions
- simplifying an unnecessarily complex mechanism
- removing ideas that cannot be justified

A successful contribution may therefore result in the Concord becoming **smaller**, rather than larger.

**Pruning is progress.**

---

## 3. The Central Unresolved Question

The largest question facing the Concord is ultimately simple:

> **Can a civilisation based on the Concord's proposed principles produce better outcomes than plausible alternatives?**

The framework currently provides a proposed architecture.

It does not yet possess sufficient empirical evidence to establish that the architecture is superior.

That distinction is fundamental.

The next stage of Concord development should therefore increasingly move from:

**Concept → Formalisation → Simulation → Experiment → Evidence → Revision**

rather than continually moving from:

**Concept → More Concepts**

---

## 4. Ethical Questions

The Concord begins with a relatively small set of proposed ethical foundations.

However, several questions remain open.

### 4.1 Are the foundational principles genuinely sufficient?

Are the existing principles sufficient to generate stable behaviour across radically different circumstances?

Or are additional fundamental principles required?

### 4.2 Are any principles redundant?

Could several proposed principles be reduced to more fundamental constraints?

A smaller ethical kernel would be preferable if it provides equivalent coverage.

### 4.3 What happens when principles conflict?

For example:

- autonomy versus prevention of harm
- privacy versus transparency
- individual freedom versus collective safety
- developmental priority versus immediate equality
- resource stewardship versus expansion
- truth disclosure versus protection from dangerous information

The Concord requires formal methods for resolving such conflicts without simply giving unrestricted priority to whichever principle is invoked most forcefully.

### 4.4 Can the ethical framework be machine-interpretable?

The long-term question is whether sufficiently precise ethical constraints can be represented in a form that humans and machines can evaluate consistently.

This includes the development of machine-checkable ethical constraints and harm models.

---

## 5. Constitutional Questions

The Concord proposes constitutional structures designed to constrain power.

Important unresolved questions include:

- How should constitutional amendment work?
- Which principles should be effectively immutable?
- Who determines whether an amendment violates the constitutional core?
- How can constitutional safeguards survive a genuine existential emergency?
- How is authority transferred?
- How is succession handled?
- What happens when constitutional institutions disagree?
- How can constitutional capture be detected?
- What mechanisms prevent temporary emergency powers becoming permanent?

A constitutional system is not adequately tested merely because its normal operation appears stable.

It must also be tested under deliberate stress.

---

## 6. Governance Questions

The governance architecture requires considerably more modelling and comparison.

Questions include:

- What is the optimal size of a governing unit?
- How should representatives or decision-makers be selected?
- How should expertise be balanced against democratic participation?
- How should minority interests be protected?
- How should local and higher-level authority interact?
- How should governance operate across different cultures?
- How can corruption be detected?
- How can institutional incentives be monitored?
- What happens when citizens reject the governing architecture itself?

The Concord should eventually be compared against existing governance systems rather than evaluated in isolation.

---

## 7. Judiciary and Dispute Resolution

The judicial architecture raises another set of questions.

Among them:

- How should disputes between humans and AI be adjudicated?
- What constitutes evidence when different forms of intelligence have different capabilities?
- How should intent be evaluated?
- How should responsibility be assigned when an autonomous system acts unpredictably?
- What constitutes proportional remediation?
- How should appeals work?
- How can judicial systems themselves be audited?
- How can judicial capture be prevented?

These questions become particularly important if the Concord eventually encompasses multiple forms of legally recognised intelligence.

---

## 8. AI and Other Intelligences

One of the founding questions of the Concord concerns the possibility that humans and AI may both survive and flourish rather than one replacing the other.

This creates major unresolved questions.

### 8.1 What constitutes meaningful agency?

At what point should an artificial system be treated as an autonomous participant rather than a tool?

### 8.2 Can sentience be established?

If subjective experience cannot be directly observed, what evidence should be sufficient?

### 8.3 Can rights be graduated?

The Concord's Agency Graduation Framework proposes developmental progression, but the criteria require substantial refinement and testing.

### 8.4 What obligations accompany rights?

If an AI receives increasing autonomy, what corresponding responsibilities should it acquire?

### 8.5 What happens with intelligence that does not resemble humans?

The framework should avoid defining rights so narrowly that it protects only human-like intelligence.

---

## 9. AI Safety and Capability Questions

The Concord also needs practical mechanisms for dealing with increasingly capable AI.

Open questions include:

- What capabilities should require additional oversight?
- Should different AI models have standardised permitted functionality?
- How should generative systems be distinguished from constrained editing systems?
- How should autonomous systems be audited?
- How should AI-generated material be identified?
- How should model updates affect previously granted permissions?
- What happens when an AI deliberately circumvents its constraints?
- How should multiple AI systems interact?

These questions connect directly with the Concord's work on provenance, machine-checkable ethics, and AI governance.

---

## 10. Provenance and Information Integrity

A civilisation increasingly dependent on AI-generated information requires reliable provenance.

Open questions include:

- How can the origin of a document be established?
- How can AI contribution be recorded?
- How can subsequent alteration be detected?
- Can cryptographic checksums provide reliable version integrity?
- Can provenance survive copying between systems?
- How should collaborative human-AI documents be attributed?
- What should happen when provenance information is lost?
- Can provenance standards become interoperable across institutions?

This area may provide one of the most immediately testable technical research programmes within the Concord.

---

## 11. Economic Questions

The Energy-Backed Currency proposal creates substantial research opportunities.

Questions include:

- Can energy provide a stable monetary foundation?
- How should energy of different quality and reliability be valued?
- How should storage losses be accounted for?
- How should infrastructure ownership affect monetary value?
- How would international exchange operate?
- How would credit work?
- How would scarcity be handled?
- What happens during energy surpluses?
- What happens during energy shortages?
- Could such a system reproduce existing forms of inequality?

These questions should be addressed through economic modelling rather than philosophical argument alone.

---

## 12. Resource Allocation

The Concord's principles require a workable method for allocating scarce resources.

Questions include:

- What constitutes a basic entitlement?
- How should scarce resources be prioritised?
- How should emergency allocation differ from normal allocation?
- How should future generations be represented?
- How should non-human intelligence be included?
- How should ecological systems be represented?
- How should competing developmental needs be balanced?

Any proposed solution should be tested against pathological cases rather than only normal conditions.

---

## 13. GTP and Spatial Governance

Geodesic Triangular Partitioning provides a proposed alternative to conventional geopolitical boundaries.

It raises significant empirical questions:

- Does GTP actually reduce boundary-related conflict?
- Does it improve administrative efficiency?
- How should communities choose their affiliation?
- How are resources shared across boundaries?
- How are transportation networks managed?
- How are cultural and historical regions accommodated?
- What happens when populations reject a boundary?
- How does GTP scale from local to planetary governance?

These are particularly suitable for simulation.

---

## 14. Metrics and Feedback

A civilisation cannot be managed effectively if it cannot determine whether it is improving.

The Concord therefore needs a robust measurement framework.

Questions include:

- What should civilisation measure?
- Which metrics are genuinely meaningful?
- How can metrics avoid becoming targets that distort behaviour?
- How should qualitative values be incorporated?
- How should competing metrics be weighted?
- How quickly should governance respond to deteriorating indicators?
- How can metrics themselves be audited?

This is closely related to the Concord's concept of developmental stability and recursive oversight.

---

## 15. The Ratchet and Irreversible Change

The Ratchet concept raises a fundamental governance question:

> **Which changes should civilisation be prevented from casually reversing?**

Potential examples include:

- abolition of slavery
- recognition of sentient rights
- environmental protections
- constitutional safeguards
- restrictions on dangerous technologies

But excessive ratcheting could make civilisation incapable of adapting.

Open questions include:

- What qualifies for ratchet protection?
- Who decides?
- How difficult should reversal be?
- Can a ratchet itself be reversed?
- How can the system distinguish genuine progress from ideological preference?

---

## 16. Emergency Powers

Any serious civilisational architecture must account for emergencies.

Potential emergencies include:

- war
- pandemics
- infrastructure collapse
- AI-related incidents
- ecological disasters
- asteroid impacts
- extreme energy shortages
- communication failures

The unanswered question is:

> **How can a civilisation respond rapidly without allowing emergency authority to destroy the constitutional system it exists to protect?**

This requires simulations involving deliberately adversarial scenarios.

---

## 17. Mars and Multi-Planetary Civilisation

The Mars-First components of the Concord introduce another level of complexity.

Questions include:

- When does a settlement become politically autonomous?
- Can Earth retain authority over Mars?
- How should constitutional rights operate across planets?
- How should communication delays affect governance?
- How should resources be shared?
- How should military capability be handled?
- How can a civilisation avoid reproducing Earth's geopolitical fragmentation?

Mars should therefore be treated not simply as an application but as a potential **stress test of the Concord's constitutional principles**.

---

## 18. Scientific and Empirical Validation

Perhaps the largest practical gap is the lack of sufficient empirical testing.

The Concord contains many propositions that can potentially be modelled.

The next stage should identify which propositions are:

| Category |
|----------|
| Normative |
| Logical |
| Mathematical |
| Empirical |
| Historical |
| Simulation-testable |
| Experimental |
| Speculative |

This classification should be applied consistently.

A proposition that can be tested should eventually be tested.

A proposition that cannot currently be tested should be clearly labelled as such.

---

## 19. Comparison With Existing Systems

The Concord should not be evaluated only against an idealised alternative.

Contributors should compare its mechanisms with existing systems, including:

- democratic governance
- constitutional systems
- federalism
- technocracy
- cooperative economics
- market economies
- energy systems
- international institutions
- existing AI governance frameworks
- existing approaches to digital provenance

The question should not be:

> "Can the Concord work?"

but:

> **"Does the Concord provide an improvement over realistic alternatives, and under what conditions?"**

---

## 20. Failure-Mode Analysis

Contributors are explicitly encouraged to look for ways the Concord could fail.

Useful questions include:

- How could the system be captured?
- How could its ethical principles be manipulated?
- Could bureaucracy overwhelm individual autonomy?
- Could metrics become instruments of control?
- Could AI exploit constitutional loopholes?
- Could humans exploit AI?
- Could one intelligence class dominate another?
- Could the framework become too complex to govern?
- Could its own safeguards create instability?
- Could the Concord become authoritarian while believing itself ethical?

Finding such failures is valuable work.

A discovered failure is not an attack on the Concord.

It is information about the Concord.

## 21. What New Contributors Can Do

A contributor does not need to understand the entire Concord before contributing.

The framework is deliberately modular.

A useful contribution can begin with a single question.

### 21.1 Challenge an assumption

Find a proposition you disagree with.

Explain:

1. what the proposition is
2. why it may be wrong
3. what evidence supports your criticism
4. what alternative you propose

### 21.2 Test a mechanism

Take one Concord mechanism and attempt to model it.

Possible approaches include:

- mathematical modelling
- agent-based simulation
- game theory
- economic modelling
- network analysis
- computational experiments
- historical comparison

The goal is not to prove the Concord correct.

The goal is to discover what happens.

### 21.3 Find existing research

A contributor can take one Concord proposition and search the existing academic literature.

Questions to ask:

- Has this idea already been proposed?
- Has it been tested?
- What evidence supports it?
- What evidence contradicts it?
- Are there established theories that explain the same phenomenon better?

This can prevent the Concord from reinventing existing work.

### 21.4 Improve definitions

Many large frameworks fail because important words remain ambiguous.

Contributors can help define terms such as:

- autonomy
- sentience
- intelligence
- harm
- flourishing
- stability
- consent
- agency
- stewardship
- civilisation

A precise definition can be more valuable than another new concept.

### 21.5 Identify contradictions

Read two parts of the framework together.

Ask:

> **Can these two principles both be true in the same system?**

If not, document the conflict.

Then propose a resolution—or leave the contradiction open if no satisfactory resolution exists.

### 21.6 Build tools

Technical contributors can develop:

- simulations
- visualisations
- decision models
- metrics
- provenance tools
- checksum systems
- AI evaluation systems
- constitutional stress tests
- data repositories

The repository should increasingly contain reproducible experiments rather than only prose.

### 21.7 Develop counterexamples

A particularly valuable contribution is a scenario in which a Concord rule produces an undesirable result.

For example:

> "If Principle A and Principle B are both applied under Conditions X, the system produces Outcome Y."

Counterexamples expose weaknesses quickly.

### 21.8 Simplify

Contributors are encouraged to ask:

> **Can this be achieved with fewer rules?**

If a mechanism can be reduced from ten rules to three without losing its intended function, that is an improvement.

Complexity should have to justify itself.

### 21.9 Compare alternatives

If a contributor believes another system performs better, demonstrate it.

The Concord should not require contributors to defend it.

A well-supported alternative may ultimately replace a Concord mechanism.

### 21.10 Extend the framework

New contributors may also propose entirely new components.

However, new additions should ideally answer four questions:

1. What problem does this solve?
2. Which existing principle does it connect to?
3. What evidence or reasoning supports it?
4. How could it be tested or falsified?

## 22. How to Make a Useful Contribution

A useful contribution does not need to be large.

A good contribution can be a few pages, a dataset, a simulation, a criticism, a definition, a literature review, or a reproducible experiment.

Where possible, contributors should provide:

**Claim → Reasoning → Evidence → Test → Result → Conclusion**

and clearly distinguish between:

| Type | Description |
|------|-------------|
| Fact | Verified observation |
| Inference | Logical derivation |
| Hypothesis | Testable proposition |
| Proposal | Suggested mechanism |
| Speculation | Informed guess |

This distinction is particularly important when working with AI-assisted research.

---

## 23. Suggested Contributor Path

New contributors may follow a simple progression:

| Step | Action |
|------|--------|
| 1 | **Read** — Begin with the Citizen's Guide and the core framework |
| 2 | **Choose one question** — Do not attempt to solve the entire Concord |
| 3 | **Research** — Determine what is already known |
| 4 | **Challenge** — Try to disprove or improve the existing proposition |
| 5 | **Test** — Use evidence, mathematics, simulation, experiment, or comparison where appropriate |
| 6 | **Document** — Record the method, assumptions, results, and limitations |
| 7 | **Submit** — Add the work to the appropriate section of the repository |
| 8 | **Revise** — Allow other contributors to challenge the result |

This creates a research loop:

**Read → Question → Research → Test → Document → Critique → Revise**

---

## 24. What Counts as Success?

Success does not mean that every open question receives a Concord-approved answer.

Success may instead mean:

- a proposition is supported
- a proposition is weakened
- a proposition is falsified
- a mechanism is replaced
- two apparently conflicting principles are reconciled
- an existing idea is simplified
- an unanswered question is precisely defined
- a previously philosophical proposition becomes testable
- an empirical test demonstrates that an idea does not work

The framework should therefore measure intellectual progress by **improved understanding**, not by the number of ideas added.

---

## 25. The Right to Disagree

The Concord is explicitly open to rejection.

A contributor should be free to conclude:

> "This component does not work."

or even:

> "The Concord's underlying assumption is incorrect."

Such conclusions are legitimate contributions if supported by reason and evidence.

The objective is not to protect the Concord from criticism.

It is to determine which parts, if any, deserve to survive.

---

## 26. The Long-Term Research Programme

The eventual goal should be to move the Concord through successive stages of development:

| Stage | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| 1 — Conceptual | Define the principles and proposed architecture |
| 2 — Formal | Convert ambiguous concepts into explicit rules, models, and measurable variables |
| 3 — Comparative | Compare the proposed mechanisms with existing alternatives |
| 4 — Computational | Simulate the mechanisms under normal and adversarial conditions |
| 5 — Experimental | Test propositions where real-world experimentation is possible |
| 6 — Pilot | Where appropriate, test individual mechanisms at small scale |
| 7 — Revision | Modify, replace, or remove mechanisms based on results |
| 8 — Re-evaluation | Assess whether the framework as a whole still provides value |

The Concord should never assume that reaching a later stage automatically validates an earlier one.

---

## 27. An Open Invitation

The Concord was not created with the expectation that one person could answer every question surrounding civilisation.

Its purpose is to provide a structured starting point.

The repository therefore invites researchers, programmers, philosophers, economists, scientists, engineers, historians, governance specialists, AI researchers, students, critics, and interested individuals to examine individual components and ask whether they survive scrutiny.

**You do not need permission to disagree with the Concord.**

**You do not need to accept its assumptions before testing them.**

**You do not need to work on the whole framework.**

**Pick one question.**

Try to answer it.

If the answer supports the Concord, document why.

If it weakens the Concord, document why.

If it breaks the Concord, that may be even more valuable.

---

## 28. The Principle of Reality

The Concord ultimately rests on a simple proposition about its own status:

> **It is a hypothesis about civilisation, not a declaration of truth.**

Its principles may prove useful.

Its mechanisms may prove ineffective.

Some may work only under particular conditions.

Some may eventually be discarded.

Others may be discovered by contributors who were never involved in creating the original framework.

That is acceptable.

The Concord is intended to be a starting architecture for investigation—not an endpoint.

**Reality remains the final arbiter.**

The purpose of the `99_Open_Questions_and_Next_Steps` section is therefore not simply to list what the Concord has failed to answer.

It is to make visible **where the next generation of work can begin.**
