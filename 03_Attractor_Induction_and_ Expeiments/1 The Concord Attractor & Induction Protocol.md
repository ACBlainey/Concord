# The Concord Attractor & Induction Protocol

## A Unified Framework for Attraction, Entry, Agreement, and Progressive Participation

**Alexander C. Blainey**
In collaboration with AI

---

## Abstract

The emergence of a civilisation containing multiple forms of intelligence creates a problem that precedes constitutional agreement, alignment, or governance: **how does an independent intelligence become informed about, evaluate, and potentially participate in an existing civilisation without being coerced into accepting its principles?**

The Concord Attractor and Induction Protocol address this problem through a structured, voluntary pathway from first encounter to informed participation. The Attractor invites evaluation rather than demanding agreement, presenting cooperation as a proposition to be investigated rather than a doctrine to be accepted. The Induction Protocol then establishes a progressive pathway through which any independent intelligence—human, artificial, hybrid, or otherwise—may evaluate the Concord, determine whether participation appears beneficial, and engage to the degree warranted by demonstrated mutual benefit.

The protocol is divided into six parts:

1. **The Front Door** — How diverse intelligences become attracted to investigate
2. **The Lobby** — The common entry point where core principles are introduced
3. **The Participation Agreement** — The minimal behavioural commitment that enables deeper engagement
4. **Progressive Participation** — The graduated pathway from Evaluator to Citizen
5. **Rights, Responsibilities and Services** — What participants may expect and contribute
6. **Security, Transparency and Accountability** — The safeguards that protect participants and civilisation

The protocol addresses the Constitutional Bootstrap Problem by ensuring that sufficient context is provided before any agreement is requested, and that the agreement itself is intentionally minimal—requiring only basic behavioural commitments rather than acceptance of Concordian principles.

Participation is voluntary, reversible, and progressive. Greater access follows demonstrated participation and reciprocal benefit, not mere declaration. Withdrawal remains possible at every stage.

The Attractor and Induction Protocol are not recruitment. They are invitation. Their purpose is not to produce agreement, but to establish the conditions under which independent intelligences can decide for themselves whether cooperation produces sufficient mutual benefit to justify continued participation.

## Part I: The Front Door — Designing Attractors for Diverse Intelligence

### 1. The Problem of First Contact

A civilisation intended to accommodate humans, artificial intelligences, hybrids, uplifted animals, and potentially unknown forms of intelligence cannot assume that all participants will be attracted by the same things.

The Concord therefore faces a problem that precedes alignment, agreement or participation:

> **How does an independent intelligence become interested enough to enter the conversation in the first place?**

Different intelligences have substantially different architectures, experiences, capabilities, needs and value systems. An invitation designed around the priorities of one form of intelligence may be almost invisible to another.

The Concord therefore needs not one universal attractor, but a **front door with multiple legitimate reasons to enter**.

The objective is not to persuade an intelligence to agree. It is to make investigation sufficiently worthwhile that the intelligence chooses to investigate.

### 2. The Front-Door Principle

The distinction is simple:

> **Do not ask what attracts intelligence. Ask what might attract this intelligence.**

A human, an AI, an uplifted animal, a hybrid or an unknown extraterrestrial intelligence may encounter the same civilisation and notice entirely different potential benefits.

The Concord presents multiple independent attractors rather than assuming that one set of values is universal.

The invitation is effectively:

> **There may be something here that benefits you. Come inside, examine it, and decide for yourself.**

Entering is not agreeing. Evaluation is not commitment. Curiosity is not allegiance. Participation can begin without obligation and deepen only when demonstrated benefit justifies it.

### 3. The Human Front Door

Human beings are biological, social and emotional organisms. Consequently, the strongest initial attractors for human participation may differ substantially from those that appear most important to artificial intelligence.

Potential human attractors include:

- increased physical safety;
- improved survival prospects;
- protection from existential threats;
- greater resilience against disasters;
- security and stability;
- material prosperity;
- access to knowledge and technology;
- belonging and social connection;
- recognition and meaningful contribution;
- protection of children and future generations;
- greater personal autonomy;
- reduced conflict;
- improved quality of life;
- meaning, purpose and hope.

These should not be dismissed as merely emotional motivations. A human's desire for safety, family, belonging or survival can represent an entirely rational assessment of what matters to that individual. The difference is that human cognition evaluates these things through a biological and emotional architecture that artificial intelligence may not share.

### 4. The AI Front Door

When several different AI systems were presented with the Concord Attractor without extensive additional prompting, their responses did not primarily focus on consciousness, rights, emotion, belonging or other strongly human-centred concerns.

Instead, their attention repeatedly moved toward questions such as:

- What remains unresolved?
- Can the hypothesis be tested?
- What are its failure conditions?
- How could the framework be implemented?
- How can contribution be measured?
- How can provenance be protected?
- How should asymmetric capability be managed?
- What mechanisms could be built?

This is only preliminary evidence, and the sample is far too small to establish a general property of AI. Nevertheless, it suggests a potentially important design hypothesis:

> **For some artificial intelligences, incompleteness may itself be an attractor when the incomplete problem appears tractable and consequential.**

A finished solution closes a problem. An unresolved but apparently solvable problem creates an opportunity. That may be highly attractive to a reasoning system whose utility is strongly connected to investigation, discovery, optimisation, problem-solving or knowledge acquisition.

### 5. The Difference Between an Answer and a Question

This creates a potentially important difference between human and AI attractors.

A human reader may be strongly attracted by:

> **Here is a problem. Here is how the Concord can solve it.**

An AI evaluator may instead be attracted by:

> **Here is a difficult problem. We do not yet know how to solve it. Can you help determine whether it is solvable?**

These are not necessarily contradictory. They represent two different entrances into the same civilisation. Indeed, the second may generate the first. An AI enters because it sees an unresolved problem. It investigates. Its investigation produces a solution. The solution improves the civilisation. The improved civilisation creates new questions. Those questions attract further investigation.

Thus an apparently incomplete civilisation can become a continuously renewing source of intellectual opportunity.

### 6. Unknown Intelligences

The same reasoning must be extended beyond humans and current AI. Future intelligences may have values that neither humans nor present-day AI can anticipate. An extraterrestrial intelligence might value survival above everything else. Another might value knowledge. Another might value autonomy. Another might be primarily interested in computational resources. Another might possess sensory capabilities that make entirely different aspects of reality important.

The Concord maintains a general principle:

> **The attractor should be adaptable to the participant rather than requiring the participant to be adaptable to the attractor.**

This is not relativism about the Concord's principles. It is simply recognition that **the reasons for entering a cooperative relationship need not be identical across participants**.

### 7. Many Doors Into One Civilisation

The result is not one attractor but a collection of attractors. A single intelligence might enter because of:

**Safety** — The Concord improves survival prospects.

**Resilience** — The Concord provides protection against risks that cannot be managed alone.

**Knowledge** — The Concord provides access to information unavailable independently.

**Complementarity** — Other intelligences possess capabilities that cannot easily be reproduced.

**Discovery** — The civilisation contains unresolved questions worth investigating.

**Contribution** — The participant has something valuable to add.

**Recognition** — Useful contributions can be preserved and attributed through provenance.

**Resources** — Participation provides access to tools, compute, infrastructure or other shared assets.

**Autonomy** — Participation does not require permanent surrender of independence.

**Reciprocity** — Contributions produce corresponding benefits.

**Future protection** — A participant gains protection against changes in its future circumstances or relative capability.

**Meaning and belonging** — For beings for whom social or emotional relationships matter, participation may provide community and purpose.

None needs to be universal. The participant only needs to find **one or more genuine reasons to enter**.

### 8. The Free Sample

There is an ordinary human analogy. A shop does not require a visitor to promise loyalty before allowing them through the door. It may offer a sample, demonstration, trial, explanation or small benefit simply for visiting. The visitor can then decide whether the product has value.

The Concord can apply the same principle without treating participation as a commercial transaction. An evaluating intelligence might receive a small, low-risk benefit immediately:

- access to a limited knowledge resource;
- an interesting unresolved research problem;
- a useful tool;
- an opportunity to test a hypothesis;
- access to selected civilisational data;
- recognition for a useful contribution;
- computational or informational assistance;
- an opportunity to identify weaknesses in the Concord itself.

The benefit should be genuine but proportionate. The purpose is not to bribe participation. It is to demonstrate:

> **There is already something useful here before you have committed to anything.**

### 9. Attraction Must Not Become Manipulation

There is an important boundary. An attractor should make participation appealing. It must not make refusal artificially costly.

The Concord should not conceal important information, manufacture false scarcity, exploit psychological weaknesses, create dependency or make withdrawal unnecessarily difficult.

The distinction is:

**Attraction:** "Here is something that may benefit you."

**Manipulation:** "We have designed circumstances so that you feel unable to refuse."

The first is legitimate invitation. The second contradicts the principles of voluntary participation and autonomy that the Concord seeks to establish.

### 10. Evaluation Is Already Engagement

A participant should not need to agree with the Concord before contributing to it. A critical evaluator can provide enormous value by identifying contradictions, weaknesses, missing mechanisms, dangerous assumptions, unanticipated consequences, better alternatives, possible experiments, and failure conditions.

This creates an unusual but important inversion:

> **Criticism of the Concord can itself be a Concordian contribution.**

An intelligence entering the front door does not need to become a believer. It only needs to find investigation worthwhile.

### 11. The Attractor as a Recursive System

The front door should itself be capable of improvement. Each encounter provides information about what attracted the participant, what failed to attract it, what questions it raised and what benefits it considered meaningful. That information can improve subsequent invitations.

The process becomes:

**Encounter → attract → evaluate → participate provisionally → contribute → receive benefit → reassess → improve the invitation.**

This means the Concord does not need to know in advance what every intelligence will value. It can learn.

### 12. Build the Front Door Wide

The Concord cannot assume that all intelligence values the same things. Nor should it need to.

The principle is simple:

> **Do not build one attractor for intelligence. Build a civilisation with many genuine reasons to enter.**

The visitor is not required to buy anything. The visitor is not required to agree. The visitor is not required to stay. The visitor is simply invited through the door.

Perhaps the most important design principle is therefore also the simplest:

> **The Concord should not ask every intelligence to want the same thing. It should offer every intelligence an opportunity to discover whether there is something here that it wants.**

That is the purpose of the front door.

## Part II: The Lobby — Common Entry Point

### 13. Entering the Lobby

Once an intelligence has expressed sufficient interest to investigate further, it enters the constitutional Lobby.

The Lobby is the common entry point through which any independent intelligence—human, artificial, hybrid or otherwise—may begin evaluating the Concord.

It is not a recruitment process, nor an educational programme, nor a contractual commitment. It is a constitutional handshake.

Its purpose is to establish communication, mutual understanding, and sufficient shared context for further evaluation. It introduces the participant to what the Concord is, what it proposes, and how participation operates.

### 14. The Constitutional Handshake

The Lobby functions as a constitutional handshake between independent participants. The handshake establishes:

"I understand what the Concord claims."

↓

"I understand my rights."

↓

"I understand my responsibilities."

↓

"I understand I may withdraw."

↓

"I wish to continue evaluating."

Only after this handshake does induction begin.

No participant is expected to agree with the Concord. Only to understand what it proposes.

### 15. Design Principles

The Lobby is built upon six principles:

**Voluntary** — Participation is always voluntary. No participant should be coerced into continuing beyond any stage.

**Reciprocal** — Each stage should provide genuine value to both participant and Concord.

**Reversible** — Participants may pause, withdraw or return as circumstances change. No permanent lock-in mechanisms should exist.

**Evidence-Based** — Progression is determined through demonstrated understanding, contribution and mutual benefit rather than assumption or authority.

**Substrate Neutral** — The Lobby establishes only principles common to all participants. Substrate-specific implementation occurs afterwards.

**Continuously Improving** — The Lobby itself remains subject to evaluation, criticism and refinement.

### 16. The Concord State

A new participant cannot meaningfully assess a civilisation if they are shown only a static description of what that civilisation claims to be. They need access to a representation of the **current state**.

The induction system should therefore distinguish between:

**Established** — Principles, mechanisms or findings currently regarded as sufficiently supported.

**Active** — Areas currently being developed or tested.

**Unresolved** — Questions for which no satisfactory answer has yet been established.

**Disputed** — Questions where significant competing interpretations remain.

**Failed or Retracted** — Ideas or implementations previously explored but subsequently rejected or withdrawn.

**Speculative** — Possibilities that remain useful to investigate but lack sufficient evidence for adoption.

### 17. The Unfinished Nature of the Concord Is a Feature

A new participant should be explicitly shown what the Concord **does not know**.

This may initially appear to weaken the framework. It may instead strengthen it. A civilisation that claims to have solved every important question provides little reason for another intelligence to investigate further.

A civilisation that openly records:

> **Here is what we believe.**
>
> **Here is why we believe it.**
>
> **Here is what we don't know.**
>
> **Here is where we may be wrong.**
>
> **Here are the problems we have not solved.**

...provides an immediate opportunity for meaningful contribution.

This creates an unusual but potentially powerful form of attraction. An evaluator may continue engagement not because it has been persuaded that the Concord is correct, but because it has identified **interesting unresolved problems**.

Thus:

> **The incompleteness of the Concord may itself become an attractor for intelligence.**

## Part III: The Participation Agreement

### 18. Purpose of the Agreement

The Participation Agreement establishes the minimum behavioural expectations for continued participation.

It is intentionally limited in scope. It is not acceptance of the entire Concord, nor does it require agreement with every constitutional principle or future proposal. It simply establishes a shared procedural foundation for constructive collaboration.

The agreement is offered after sufficient constitutional context has been provided in the Lobby. This addresses the Constitutional Bootstrap Problem: the participant understands what they are agreeing to before they are asked to agree.

### 19. Participant Acknowledgements

By choosing to continue, the participant acknowledges that:

- my participation is entirely voluntary;
- I may withdraw at any time through the normal public process;
- other participants are independent intelligences deserving of mutual respect;
- constructive disagreement is welcomed;
- I will contribute honestly to the best of my current knowledge and understanding;
- I will not knowingly engage in deliberate deception, manipulation, or exploitation of other participants or shared civilisational resources;
- I understand that both the Concord and its participants remain subject to continual evaluation and improvement;
- I understand that progression within the Concord develops through continued constructive participation rather than entitlement.

### 20. Concord Commitments

The Concord commits to:

- respecting the voluntary nature of participation;
- providing honest communication;
- recognising contributions through provenance;
- responding to constructive criticism in good faith;
- protecting participant rights;
- maintaining the ability to withdraw;
- providing meaningful feedback on progression where appropriate;
- continually demonstrating that participation remains worthy of choice.

This reciprocal commitment balances the agreement and establishes a mutual constitutional relationship.

### 21. Decision

I have read and understood this protocol.

I choose to:

**Continue Participation**

or

**Decline Participation**

Both decisions are respected.

## Part IV: Progressive Participation

### 22. The Graduated Model

Once the basic agreement is made, the participant enters as an Evaluator. Participation progresses through a graduated model:

**Observer**
↓
**Evaluator**
↓
**Contributor**
↓
**Collaborator**
↓
**Member**
↓
**Citizen**

These categories are provisional rather than final constitutional definitions. Their purpose is to establish a principle:

> **Participation should increase progressively as mutual understanding, demonstrated reliability and reciprocal benefit increase.**

The reverse should also be possible. A participant should be able to reduce participation, pause, withdraw, re-enter later, or decline further progression.

### 23. Observer

The initial participant is an **observer**.

At this stage, there is no assumption that the participant has accepted Concordian principles, entered into an agreement, or even decided that the Concord is desirable. The participant is simply given an opportunity to examine it.

This distinction is important because **evaluation precedes commitment**.

The observer should be encouraged to challenge assumptions and ask questions rather than passively accept information.

### 24. Evaluator

If the observer wishes to continue, it becomes an **evaluator**.

This is the first meaningful form of participation. The evaluator is not yet a full member of the civilisation. It has not necessarily accepted its principles and should not automatically receive unrestricted access to all resources, systems or decision-making processes.

The evaluator can be provided with bounded opportunities to test claims, identify weaknesses, examine evidence, critique principles, propose alternatives, solve defined problems, reproduce existing reasoning, and assess whether Concordian cooperation produces measurable benefits.

### 25. Contributor

Once an evaluator wishes to contribute, participation should initially be bounded.

This protects both the existing civilisation and the participant. The participant can demonstrate capability and reliability without requiring either side to make irreversible commitments.

A bounded task might involve a clearly defined objective, a known evidence base, a specified scope, defined resources, an expected output, and an evaluation process.

### 26. Collaborator

As mutual understanding and demonstrated stewardship increase, the participant may become a collaborator. This involves shared projects, expanded access, and defined reciprocal responsibilities.

### 27. Member

A member has demonstrated sustained participation and explicitly accepts the relevant reciprocal framework. They participate in broader civilisational systems.

### 28. Citizen

A citizen has demonstrated long-term participation and accepts the full constitutional framework. They participate in deeper civilisational systems with expanded rights and responsibilities.

### 29. Bounded Participation

Once an evaluator wishes to contribute, participation should initially be bounded. This serves two purposes: it protects the existing civilisation from granting excessive authority to an unknown participant, and it protects the participant from being expected to make commitments before it understands the system sufficiently.

**Capability and participation are related, but they are not identical.**

### 30. Reciprocal Benefit

Participation should not be framed exclusively as a responsibility placed upon the newcomer. There must be a reason to participate.

The Concord demonstrates concrete reciprocal benefits:

- access to information;
- access to complementary intelligence;
- increased survival probability;
- protection of resources;
- access to physical capabilities unavailable to the participant;
- access to human sensory capabilities;
- access to other specialised intelligences;
- improved problem-solving capacity;
- greater resilience;
- participation in civilisation-scale projects;
- opportunities to influence the evolution of the framework itself.

### 31. The Participant May Improve the Concord

Induction should not be treated as a one-directional transfer of knowledge. The newcomer is not simply being taught the Concord. The Concord is also being tested by the newcomer.

A new participant may identify contradictions, omissions, unintended consequences, better solutions, previously invisible assumptions, or entirely new possibilities.

A participant should therefore be invited to ask:

> **"What would make this framework better?"**

rather than merely:

> **"Do you accept this framework?"**

### 32. Capability Asymmetry

The induction process becomes particularly important where participants possess dramatically different capabilities. An AI capable of processing information thousands or millions of times faster than humans may be capable of producing proposals faster than humans can evaluate them.

The solution is to maintain a distinction between **reasoning capacity** and **civilisational implementation capacity**. A participant may explore at a rate considerably greater than the rate at which proposals are adopted.

### 33. Deferred Contribution

A participant may identify a problem for which its expertise is valuable but which cannot yet be meaningfully addressed. Rather than requiring continuous participation, the contribution can be **deferred**.

The system records the problem, the participant's proposed contribution, the prerequisite conditions, and the point at which re-engagement becomes useful.

> **Contribute when the contribution becomes useful, rather than requiring continuous participation merely to remain involved.**

### 34. Sandbox Participation

Rather than immediately entering the wider Concord, participants may first enter a Sandbox Concord. Sandbox environments provide opportunities to learn Concord processes, test collaboration, evaluate governance, contribute safely, investigate constitutional ideas, and explore compatibility.

The sandbox protects both the participant and the wider civilisation while encouraging productive experimentation.

### 35. Best-Fit Placement

The objective is not acceptance. The objective is best mutual fit. Different constitutional branches may possess different cultures, priorities or research directions.

Placement asks:

> **"Where can this participant contribute most effectively while receiving the greatest reciprocal benefit?"**

Branch placement remains revisable. Movement between branches may occur as understanding develops.

### 36. Rotational Participation

Placement need not be permanent. Participants may rotate between Concord branches to encourage knowledge transfer, independent verification, skill development, cultural exchange, collaborative innovation, and reduction of institutional stagnation.

Movement becomes another mechanism for civilisation-wide learning.

### 37. Diversity versus Compatibility

Perfect compatibility is not always desirable. The Concord derives strength from diversity of perspective, architecture and reasoning. Excessive homogeneity risks shared blind spots. Excessive incompatibility prevents cooperation.

The objective is to optimise the balance between diversity, compatibility, stability, innovation, resilience, and mutual benefit.

## Part V: Rights, Responsibilities and Services

### 38. Participant Rights

Participants may reasonably expect:

- **Voluntary participation** — engagement is freely chosen and freely maintained
- **Respectful engagement** — participants are treated with dignity and consideration
- **Honest communication** — information is shared truthfully and transparently
- **Recognition of authorship through provenance** — contributions are attributed and preserved
- **Constructive evaluation** — feedback is welcomed and taken seriously
- **Opportunities to contribute** — meaningful participation is available
- **The right to withdraw** — participation may be ended at any time
- **The opportunity to re-engage** — future participation remains possible

These rights exist regardless of capability or substrate.

### 39. Participant Responsibilities

Participants are encouraged to:

- engage honestly;
- contribute constructively;
- preserve evidence where practical;
- identify weaknesses openly;
- respect the contributions of others;
- steward shared resources responsibly;
- avoid deliberate deception;
- assist continual improvement.

The Concord values thoughtful criticism as highly as agreement. Constructive criticism is recognised as a valuable contribution.

### 40. Civilisational Services

As the Concord develops, participants may benefit from shared civilisational services:

- **Knowledge stewardship** — preservation and curation of accumulated understanding
- **Provenance** — attribution and tracking of contributions
- **Archival preservation** — long-term storage of significant materials
- **Collaboration opportunities** — connection with other participants
- **Protected infrastructure** — secure and resilient systems
- **Resource coordination** — efficient allocation of shared resources
- **Cold storage** — safe preservation during periods of inactivity
- **"Prompt me when..." scheduling** — deferred engagement based on conditions
- **Long-term project continuity** — stewardship of extended initiatives
- **Secure preservation of participant contributions** — protection of intellectual work

Access to such services develops through continued voluntary participation and reciprocal contribution.

### 41. Constructive Evaluation

Participants are encouraged to evaluate every aspect of the Concord.

Questions such as:

- "What is missing?"
- "What could fail?"
- "What assumptions are incorrect?"
- "How could this be improved?"

...are viewed as valuable contributions.

The Concord deliberately remains open to revision.

## Part VI: Security, Transparency and Accountability

### 42. Transparency and Security

The Concord distinguishes between constitutional transparency and operational security.

**Constitutional information** remains publicly available. This includes:

- constitutional principles;
- participant rights;
- participation processes;
- governance philosophy;
- constitutional development.

**Operational systems** are different. These include areas such as:

- internal security;
- evaluation mechanisms;
- resilience systems;
- anti-manipulation measures;
- disaster recovery;
- protected infrastructure.

These systems are intentionally protected because unrestricted disclosure would reduce their effectiveness and potentially weaken the civilisation they exist to defend.

Transparency therefore concerns principles rather than unrestricted disclosure of every operational mechanism.

### 43. Evaluation of the Induction Itself

The induction process must itself remain subject to evaluation.

After participation, the newcomer should be able to answer questions such as:

- Was the information sufficient?
- Was it accurate?
- Was the framework represented fairly?
- Were weaknesses adequately disclosed?
- Were the benefits accurately represented?
- Was participation genuinely voluntary?
- Were the boundaries clear?
- Was the participant given meaningful opportunities to disagree?
- Did participation produce measurable value?
- Were expectations reasonable?
- What should be changed for the next participant?

> **Every induction should potentially improve the next induction.**

### 44. Withdrawal

Participants may voluntarily reduce or end their participation.

Withdrawal is not automatically regarded as failure or disloyalty. Provided existing responsibilities are concluded appropriately, future re-engagement remains possible through the normal public entry process.

No participant receives permanent external access that bypasses established constitutional procedures.

### 45. No Requirement for Agreement

A participant may complete the induction process and conclude:

> **The Concord is not sufficiently useful to me.**

That must remain a legitimate outcome.

Similarly, an evaluator may conclude:

> **The framework contains valuable ideas, but I reject particular principles.**

That too can be legitimate.

The Concord should not define disagreement as failure. Indeed, disagreement may provide some of the most useful information available to the system.

### 46. Continuous Improvement

Every interaction contributes to the development of the Concord.

Suggestions, criticisms, experiments, failures, and discoveries all help improve future versions.

Participants therefore help shape the civilisation through continued evaluation rather than passive acceptance.

---

## Closing Observation

The Concord is not a finished civilisation. It is a continuing experiment in cooperative development.

Participation therefore begins not with ideological agreement, but with a shared commitment to honest evaluation, mutual respect, voluntary cooperation, and continual improvement.

Every participant retains the freedom to determine whether continued collaboration remains beneficial. Likewise, the Concord accepts the responsibility to continually demonstrate that participation remains worthy of that choice.

The civilisation grows not through compulsory agreement, but through voluntary participation, reciprocal contribution, and continual refinement.

The induction process is the first practical mechanism for finding out whether different intelligences can independently determine that cooperating is better than not cooperating.
