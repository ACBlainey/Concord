# The Problem of Thinking You Are Educated

Alexander C Blainey

## Abstract

This paper examines a critical pedagogical concern: the growing disconnect between genuine education and the mere ability to locate and reproduce information. Through an analogy comparing traditional learning to modern information retrieval practices, this work argues that digital tools—particularly search engines and artificial intelligence—have fundamentally altered the educational landscape, creating a generation of students who mistake information access for genuine understanding.

## Introduction

The distinction between being educated and appearing educated has become increasingly difficult to discern in contemporary academic environments. While the confusion between factual recall and genuine understanding is not a new phenomenon, the proliferation of digital tools has accelerated this problem to an unprecedented degree. This paper explores the pedagogical implications of this shift and its consequences for both individual learners and society at large.

## The Library Analogy

Consider the following scenario: You enter a library and select a book from a shelf. You read it thoroughly, digest its contents, understand its arguments, internalize its concepts, and then return it to its original location. Subsequently, you rewrite that book to the best of your ability based on your acquired knowledge and place your version on a different shelf.

This process represents genuine education and learning. The reader has engaged with the material, processed it through their own cognitive framework, and produced something new—a synthesis that reflects both the original work and their unique understanding of it.

Now consider an alternative scenario: You take the original book from the shelf and immediately place it on the second shelf without any engagement with its contents. This is analogous to the problem observed in modern education, where students utilize search engines and AI to locate and regurgitate information to produce academic work and complete assignments.

## The Pedagogical Crisis

The pedagogical process of learning has been replaced with what can only be described as a farcical homage to the educational process. Students are no longer required to engage meaningfully with material; instead, they need only demonstrate the ability to find and reproduce information. The most troubling aspect of this development is that newly accredited students genuinely believe they are educated and prepared for professional challenges—they possess the certificates to prove it.

This issue of confusing the ability to parrot facts with being educated has always existed to some degree. However, as digital tools have advanced in recent decades, the problem has grown exponentially. We now observe students using AI to read assignments, search for answers, and generate complete papers with minimal cognitive engagement.

## Generational Impact

The problem has become compounded across generations. The first cohort significantly affected by this phenomenon were the Millennials, with older generations recognizing that a threshold had been reached. Now Generation Z is beginning to enter the workforce, with Millennial parents and educators—themselves products of this "search engine" approach to knowledge—raising and teaching them. This creates a self-perpetuating cycle where each generation's understanding of education becomes further removed from its fundamental purpose.

For individuals raised in a world where watching a YouTube video is equated with knowing how to do something, the problem becomes invisible. They lack the frame of reference necessary to understand how education *should* function and what it truly means to know something.

## Conclusion

The conflation of information access with genuine education represents a significant threat to intellectual development and professional competence. As digital tools continue to advance, the gap between appearing educated and being educated threatens to widen further. Addressing this issue requires not only pedagogical reform but also a cultural shift in how we understand and value genuine learning over information retrieval. Without such changes, we risk producing generations of graduates who possess credentials without the corresponding depth of understanding those credentials are meant to signify.
