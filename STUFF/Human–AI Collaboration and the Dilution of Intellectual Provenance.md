# Human–AI Collaboration and the Dilution of Intellectual Provenance

## Alexander C Blainey

### Introduction

One problem I have encountered during the development of the Concord is the potential dilution of human intellectual input during an extended human–AI writing process. This issue manifests in two related but distinct forms: the progressive obscuring of intellectual provenance through recursive collaboration, and the particular challenges faced by authors with dyslexia who rely on AI for transcription and communication support. Both raise fundamental questions about authorship, attribution, and the nature of intellectual contribution in an era of increasingly sophisticated language models.

### The Iterative Writing Process

Writing is rarely a single act. My own process typically develops through a series of increasingly detailed stages:

1. **Bullet-point outlines** — identifying ideas that need to be developed.
2. **Short paragraphs** — expanding those ideas into preliminary explanations.
3. **Extended summaries** — developing the paragraphs into multi-page treatments of varying depth.
4. **Essays or papers** — expanding the material into a complete treatment of the subject.
5. **Larger papers** — combining essays and papers covering related ideas.
6. **Books** — combining multiple papers into larger works covering multiple related domains.

In my case, stages 1–3 are normally almost entirely human-generated. AI assistance at these stages may consist primarily of formatting, spelling correction and improving readability, particularly where dyslexia makes the mechanical production of text difficult. From stage 4 onward, however, the nature of the interaction can change considerably.

### The AI as Sounding Board

I will often interact with an AI system as though it were a conversational sounding board. I present an idea, discuss it, receive a response, modify the idea and continue the discussion. The original purpose of this process is not necessarily to obtain new ideas from the AI, but to externalise my own thinking and test whether the resulting argument appears coherent.

Before AI systems were available, I effectively performed the same process with my wife. I would explain an idea aloud, she would politely listen, occasionally agree, occasionally disagree, and sometimes provide the much more useful response of looking completely confused. That process nevertheless served a purpose: saying an idea aloud often reveals whether it actually makes sense.

An AI provides a vastly more responsive sounding board. It can identify contradictions, inconsistencies, tensions and gaps in an argument. This can be extremely valuable. The author can then return to an earlier stage of the process, develop the missing idea, reconsider an assumption or construct a solution. In this form, AI is functioning primarily as a **critical instrument**.

### The Boundary Crossed

But a boundary can easily be crossed. An AI may identify a problem and then attempt to provide the solution itself. At that point the provenance of the resulting idea becomes considerably less clear.

If the AI says, in effect, *"There is an inconsistency here,"* and the author develops their own solution, the intellectual contribution remains relatively straightforward to attribute. If the AI instead says, *"There is an inconsistency here, and here is the solution,"* and that solution is incorporated into the work, the situation becomes different. The question then becomes:

> **Was the resulting idea developed by the author, by the AI, or collaboratively?**

There is no simple answer. The answer depends upon the nature of the contribution.

### Progressive Substitution in Synthesis

The same problem becomes increasingly important during stages 5 and 6, when multiple papers are combined. Collation frequently exposes tensions between previously independent arguments. Two papers may contain assumptions that are individually reasonable but mutually incompatible when placed within a single framework.

Again, there are two possible responses. The tension can send the author back to the beginning of the process. The underlying assumptions can be reconsidered, new material developed and the papers revised. Alternatively, it can be tempting to ask the AI to simply resolve the contradiction and insert the resulting solution. The latter approach is considerably faster, but it creates a risk of **progressive intellectual substitution**.

It may be further confused where the AI suggests a solution the author was already considering. At each stage, a small amount of AI-generated material may appear harmless. But over many iterations, these additions can accumulate. Eventually the author may no longer have a clear memory of which conclusions originated as their own ideas, which were prompted by criticism, which were independently developed after AI feedback, and which were directly supplied by the AI.

### Recursive Processes and Provenance Drift

The problem becomes particularly serious in a recursive writing process. A typical recursive process might be:

> idea → expansion → critique → revision → expansion → critique → synthesis → further expansion → further synthesis.

If the output of one AI-assisted stage becomes the input to the next, the AI can increasingly influence the conceptual trajectory of the work. Each subsequent iteration may appear to be based upon the author's previous work, while actually containing an increasing proportion of ideas introduced by the AI during earlier iterations.

This creates a form of **provenance drift**. The final document may still feel entirely like the author's work. It may even contain no obviously identifiable passage that can be labelled "AI generated". Yet the conceptual ancestry of individual ideas may have become impossible to reconstruct.

This suggests that long-form human–AI collaboration requires more than simply recording that AI was used. It requires some mechanism for preserving **intellectual provenance** throughout the development process.

### A Guiding Principle

One possible principle is therefore:

> **The greater the degree of recursive transformation, synthesis and abstraction, the greater the need to preserve provenance of the underlying ideas.**

This does not imply that AI contributions are inherently illegitimate. On the contrary, criticism, alternative interpretations, error detection, synthesis and hypothesis generation can greatly increase the quality and scope of human intellectual work.

The important distinction is between **assistance and substitution**. An AI can help an author discover that an argument is weak without becoming the author of the replacement argument. It can identify a contradiction without being permitted to silently determine which side should prevail. It can propose possibilities without those possibilities automatically becoming conclusions.

The safest process may therefore be one in which AI-generated solutions are treated as **candidate inputs requiring explicit human adoption**, rather than as invisible additions to the author's reasoning.

This creates an important methodological discipline:

**AI may challenge the reasoning.**  
**AI may propose alternatives.**  
**AI may identify gaps.**  
**AI may generate hypotheses.**  
**But the author must remain aware of which ideas they have actually adopted, why they have adopted them, and where those ideas originated.**

### The Value of Process Recording

This is particularly important for a project such as the Concord, where the purpose is not simply to produce polished text but to preserve a developing chain of reasoning. The record of that development may itself have value. A speculative idea that is later discarded, an AI-generated suggestion that proves incorrect, a contradiction that forces a return to first principles, or a solution that emerges only after repeated human–AI interaction can all form part of the intellectual history of the project.

The objective should therefore not be to eliminate AI influence. It should be to **make that influence visible enough that human judgement, intellectual provenance and responsibility are not lost during the process of collaboration**.

### Extension: Dyslexia as a Special Case

There is an additional complication in my own case that amplifies these concerns and introduces distinct challenges.

I have often described my dyslexia to others using an analogy involving a computer connected to the internet. For most people, written language is effectively a high-bandwidth connection. Reading and writing can occur relatively directly. For me, the connection is considerably less efficient. more like a super computer connected via dial up !
Written language often feels almost like a second language. When reading, I effectively have to translate the written material internally before its meaning becomes fully accessible in my normal internal language. The reverse process occurs when writing: an internally coherent idea has to be converted into written language.

Both directions are slow, cumbersome and prone to errors. I may therefore read and reread a paragraph several times before extracting what it actually says. Likewise, I may write and rewrite the same paragraph repeatedly before it becomes readily understandable to someone else.

This creates an unusual distinction between **the quality of the thought** and **the quality of its written representation**. The thought may be coherent while the prose expressing it is not. The severity also varies considerably. At some times I can produce relatively clean prose. At others, particularly when tired or working during periods when my cognitive productivity is highest, the written output can become extremely difficult even for me to read. It is an uncomfortable but honest admission that there are times when I can have considerable difficulty reading my own freshly written material.

### The Transformative Role of LLMs

This is where modern LLMs have produced an extraordinary change in my ability to write. LLMs appear to have relatively little difficulty reconstructing the intended meaning of badly written material because they operate heavily through contextual relationships rather than simply interpreting each word independently. Consequently, I can sometimes write continuously for hours without even looking closely at the screen, knowing that the resulting material can subsequently be reconstructed into readable prose.

For me, this has effectively removed a major bottleneck between thought and publication. It has allowed ideas which previously would have remained largely internal to become written and therefore available for examination by others.

But this creates the same authorship problem in an unusually strong form. If the original thought is mine but the written expression is transformed substantially by AI, **how much of the final text belongs to the author and how much should be attributed to the AI?**

### The Sliding Scale of Contribution

There is no simple binary answer. The answer exists on a sliding scale. At one end, the AI may merely correct spelling and grammar while preserving the author's wording and structure. Further along the scale, it may reorganise fragmented thoughts into coherent prose while preserving the underlying concepts. Further still, it may identify weaknesses, propose alternatives, or introduce conceptual connections. At the extreme end, the AI may generate substantial new ideas which the author subsequently accepts and incorporates.

These are materially different forms of collaboration, even though all might superficially be described as "AI-assisted writing." This suggests that authorship should not necessarily be treated as a binary question of **"Was AI used?"** A more useful question is: **"What did the AI actually contribute?"**

### The Attribution Challenge for Disabled Authors

There is an important distinction between an AI acting as a transcription and communication aid, an editor, a critic, a synthesiser, and an intellectual contributor. This is particularly important for disabled authors.

If AI can compensate for a disability that otherwise prevents an individual from expressing their own ideas, treating all resulting prose as equivalent to AI authorship would produce an absurd outcome. At the same time, disability cannot provide an automatic justification for attributing genuinely novel AI-generated ideas to the human author.

The appropriate approach is therefore neither to minimise AI contribution nor to exaggerate it. Instead, the developmental process should remain as transparent as reasonably possible. The fundamental distinction is between **recovering and expressing a human idea** and **creating a new idea through AI participation**.

### Conclusion

In practice, the boundary between human and AI contribution will sometimes be unclear. That ambiguity is not necessarily a defect in the methodology. It is a genuine characteristic of human–AI collaborative reasoning. The more recursive and iterative the process becomes, the more important provenance becomes.

A mature approach to AI-assisted authorship may therefore require not merely identifying whether AI was used, but maintaining some record of **how the intellectual content evolved**. This is true whether the collaboration is driven by a desire for intellectual refinement, by the need for accessibility, or by a combination of both.

The objective is not to prevent collaboration. It is to prevent the collaborative process from silently obscuring where the ideas came from. Whether the AI functions primarily as a critical instrument, a transcription aid, or a generative partner, the intellectual provenance of the final work must remain sufficiently visible to support meaningful attribution and preserve human responsibility for the ideas ultimately presented.
