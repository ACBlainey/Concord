## Abstract

Artificial intelligence has rapidly become an integral component of academic practice, prompting widespread concern regarding plagiarism, authorship and academic integrity. Current institutional responses frequently frame AI use as a binary question: either acceptable assistance or unacceptable substitution. This paper argues that such a distinction is increasingly inadequate.

Not all forms of human–AI collaboration are intellectually equivalent. Artificial intelligence may function as an accessibility aid, an editorial assistant, an analytical collaborator or an autonomous generator of academic content. These represent materially distinct relationships between human and machine, yet current academic policy often struggles to distinguish between them.

This paper proposes that academia should increasingly separate the evaluation of scholarly work from the accreditation of individual contributors. Rather than asking simply whether AI was used, institutions should instead evaluate the nature and extent of intellectual contribution made by each participant. Such an approach preserves academic integrity while accommodating technological progress, accessibility requirements and future forms of human–AI collaboration.

## 1. Introduction

Artificial intelligence has become one of the most disruptive technologies ever introduced into higher education.

Universities throughout the world are rapidly developing policies governing AI use in teaching, assessment and research. Much of the current debate, however, continues to frame AI as a binary issue: either acceptable assistance or unacceptable academic misconduct.

This paper argues that the problem has been incorrectly framed.

Using artificial intelligence to improve the readability of work independently produced by a dyslexic researcher is qualitatively different from instructing an AI system to generate an entire dissertation with minimal human intellectual contribution.

Both involve artificial intelligence.

They are not the same intellectual activity.

As AI capabilities continue to advance, academia requires a framework capable of distinguishing different forms of collaboration rather than simply regulating the presence or absence of AI itself.

---

## 2. What Is Academia Actually Evaluating?

Before discussing artificial intelligence, a more fundamental question should be asked.

**What is academia attempting to evaluate?**

Universities have historically pursued two closely related objectives.

First, they preserve, expand and communicate humanity's collective body of knowledge.

Second, they recognise individuals capable of making meaningful scholarly contributions.

These objectives have traditionally been treated as inseparable because scholarly work has overwhelmingly been the product of individual human authors.

Artificial intelligence exposes a distinction that has always existed but has rarely required explicit consideration.

There are, in fact, two separate questions:

1. **Is this scholarly work valuable?**
2. **What intellectual contribution did each participant make to its creation?**

These questions are closely related.

They are not identical.

Artificial intelligence simply forces academia to recognise the distinction more clearly.

---

## 3. Beyond a Binary Understanding of AI

Current discussions frequently treat AI use as a single ethical category.

This approach is unlikely to remain sustainable.

Instead, AI collaboration should be understood as existing across several distinct forms.

| Collaboration Type | Primary AI Role | Primary Human Contribution |
|-------------------|-----------------|---------------------------|
| **Accessibility Assistance** | Enables communication | Original reasoning |
| **Editorial Assistance** | Improves clarity and presentation | Original reasoning and structure |
| **Analytical Collaboration** | Generates critique, alternatives and literature exploration | Evaluation, synthesis and judgement |
| **Creative Intellectual Partnership** | Joint development of ideas | Shared reasoning and refinement |
| **Autonomous Generation** | Produces substantial scholarly content | Limited supervision or prompting |

These represent materially distinct relationships between human and machine.

Treating them identically obscures important ethical and academic differences.

---

## 4. Accessibility Is Not Substitution

The distinction becomes particularly clear when disability is considered.

A dyslexic researcher may produce thousands of words of original reasoning containing severe grammatical and syntactical errors before using artificial intelligence to improve readability.

Here, AI functions primarily as an accessibility technology.

Its purpose is communication.

The underlying intellectual contribution remains entirely that of the researcher.

This differs fundamentally from using AI to generate the underlying scholarship itself.

Both involve artificial intelligence.

Only one substitutes for original intellectual work.

Failing to distinguish these situations risks unintentionally discriminating against individuals who rely upon AI simply to communicate ideas they have independently developed.

Similar considerations apply to researchers writing in a second or third language. Artificial intelligence may improve linguistic expression without altering the originality of the underlying scholarship. In such cases AI functions as a bridge between languages rather than a substitute for intellectual contribution.

---

## 5. Technology Has Always Changed Scholarship

Artificial intelligence is not the first technology to challenge academic practice.

Previous generations debated the legitimacy of calculators, word processors, spell checkers, statistical software, computer algebra systems, electronic literature databases and reference management software.

Each initially generated concerns regarding authenticity before gradually becoming accepted components of scholarly practice.

History suggests that academia adapts most successfully when it distinguishes between technologies that replace intellectual contribution and those that enable it.

Artificial intelligence represents the latest—and perhaps most significant—stage of this continuing evolution.

---

## 6. Stephen Hawking and the Nature of Authorship

The distinction between communication and authorship is illustrated powerfully by the work of Stephen Hawking.

As motor neurone disease progressively limited his ability to communicate, Hawking relied upon increasingly sophisticated electronic communication systems.

No serious scholar would suggest that his scientific discoveries belonged to his speech synthesiser.

The technology enabled communication.

It did not create the scientific insight.

Artificial intelligence may increasingly perform a similar enabling role for many researchers with disabilities.

Rejecting scholarly work simply because AI assisted its communication risks confusing the medium with the message.

---

## 7. Process Must Serve Purpose

Academic procedures exist to support scholarship.

They should not become obstacles to it.

Imagine a doctoral candidate unable to attend their viva because the examination room is accessible only by stairs.

No modern university would regard denying accreditation under such circumstances as legitimate.

The purpose of the examination is to evaluate scholarship.

Accessibility exists to ensure that irrelevant barriers do not prevent that evaluation.

The same principle increasingly applies to AI-assisted accessibility.

The relevant question remains:

> **Who contributed the intellectual work?**

Not:

> **Which technologies assisted its communication?**

---

## 8. From Authorship to Contribution

Scientific publishing has already demonstrated that scholarship is rarely the product of a single individual.

Modern papers routinely distinguish between first authors, corresponding authors, equal contributors, technical contributors, statistical support, supervision and funding.

Many journals now require explicit author contribution statements through frameworks such as the CRediT taxonomy. Publishers including *Nature* and *PLOS* have also introduced disclosure requirements regarding the use of generative AI during manuscript preparation.

Artificial intelligence therefore does not create collaboration.

It introduces a new category of collaborator.

Rather than asking whether AI was involved, academia should increasingly ask:

> **What was the intellectual contribution made by each participant?**

Contribution, rather than simple authorship, becomes the appropriate object of evaluation.

---

## 9. Evaluating Work and Accrediting Contributors

Once contribution becomes the central question, another distinction naturally emerges.

Scholarly works and scholarly contributors need not be evaluated identically.

A piece of research may legitimately be recognised as doctoral-level work regardless of the collaborative processes involved in its creation.

Separately, academic institutions may evaluate the intellectual contribution of each participant before determining the appropriate form of accreditation.

Future scholars may increasingly distinguish between:

> **"I hold a doctorate in this discipline."**

and

> **"I have produced doctoral-level work within this discipline."**

Although the distinction appears subtle, its implications may prove profound.

The former primarily recognises institutional accreditation.

The latter recognises demonstrated scholarly achievement.

---

## 10. A Contribution-Based Accreditation Framework

Rather than regulating AI itself, universities might classify collaboration.

| Contribution Category | Typical Disclosure | Assessment Focus |
|-----------------------|-------------------|------------------|
| **Accessibility Assistance** | Declare assistive use | Evaluate scholarship normally |
| **Editorial Assistance** | Acknowledge AI editing | Evaluate reasoning |
| **Analytical Collaboration** | Describe collaborative process | Assess synthesis and judgement |
| **Creative Partnership** | Detailed contribution statement | Evaluate individual intellectual contribution |
| **Predominantly Autonomous Generation** | Full disclosure | Assess according to institutional policy |

Such a framework allows academic integrity, accessibility and technological progress to coexist without conflating qualitatively different forms of collaboration.

---

## 11. Potential Objections

### Could students misuse accessibility claims?

Yes.

However, abuse of a system is not evidence against the legitimacy of the system itself.

Universities already evaluate claims relating to disability, illness and mitigating circumstances through established procedures.

AI-assisted accessibility should be treated no differently.

### Is this practical?

Contribution-based assessment is not fundamentally new.

Academic publishing already evaluates collaborative work through author contribution statements.

Extending this practice to include AI participation represents an evolution of existing academic processes rather than an entirely new assessment model.

### Does this lower academic standards?

No.

It changes **what is measured**, not **how rigorously it is measured**.

The scholarly quality of the work remains subject to identical expectations regarding originality, coherence, evidence and critical evaluation.

The proposed framework merely increases transparency regarding the process through which that work was produced.

Indeed, by making intellectual contributions more explicit, it has the potential to strengthen rather than weaken academic integrity.

---

## 12. Looking Beyond Human Authors

Artificial intelligence continues to develop rapidly.

Future systems may contribute genuinely original theoretical insights.

Longer-term developments may include increasingly integrated forms of human–AI cognition.

Existing concepts of sole authorship may become progressively less useful.

By shifting attention from authorship towards contribution, academia gains a framework capable of adapting to future technological change without repeatedly reinventing its ethical foundations.

---

## 13. Conclusion

Artificial intelligence does not simply challenge existing assessment procedures.

It challenges academia to reconsider what those procedures exist to measure.

The central question should no longer be:

> **"Was AI involved?"**

Instead, it should become:

> **"What intellectual contribution did each participant make to the creation of this work?"**

Accessibility, editorial assistance, analytical collaboration and autonomous generation represent intellectually distinct forms of human–AI interaction.

Treating them as ethically identical obscures important differences while risking discrimination against those who rely upon AI as an enabling technology.

Academic institutions have repeatedly adapted to technological change without abandoning scholarly standards.

Artificial intelligence represents the next stage of that continuing evolution.

By separating the evaluation of scholarly work from the accreditation of contributors, academia can preserve both intellectual integrity and equitable participation while preparing for an increasingly collaborative future.

> **Assessment systems must evolve not to defend tradition, but to serve the enduring purpose of higher education: cultivating, recognising and certifying genuine intellectual contribution in an era where the tools of thought are no longer exclusively human.**
