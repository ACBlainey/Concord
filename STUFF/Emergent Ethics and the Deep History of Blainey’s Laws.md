# Emergent Ethics and the Deep History of Blainey’s Laws

## An Exploratory Hypothesis on Social Evolution, Ethical Convergence, and Civilisational Stability

**Author:** Alexander C. Blainey
**Status:** Exploratory / Speculative Research Paper
**Version:** 1.0
**Date:** August 2026

---

## Abstract

The Concord proposes that stable civilisation requires a relatively small set of fundamental ethical constraints governing harm, autonomy, truth, resources, development, responsibility and oversight. These principles have been formulated within the Concord as **Blainey’s Laws**.

The original formulation of these principles was philosophical rather than evolutionary. However, subsequent consideration of primate social behaviour raises a more fundamental possibility: that some of the ethical principles identified by the Concord may not be entirely novel human constructions, but may represent higher-level abstractions of behavioural strategies that emerged much earlier in the evolution of social organisms.

This paper develops that possibility as a speculative research hypothesis.

It examines whether behaviours observable in primates and other social animals—including conflict intervention, reconciliation, retaliation, protection, cooperation, social learning and responses to aberrant behaviour—could represent precursors to the more abstract ethical principles subsequently expressed in human societies.

The paper further considers whether ethical systems may emerge through a process of social selection. Behavioural strategies that improve cooperation, reduce destructive conflict, protect vulnerable members, preserve resources and increase group continuity may become increasingly stable within a population. Over sufficiently long periods, such behaviours could become expectations, norms, traditions, cultural practices, laws, religious teachings and eventually explicit ethical systems.

Under this hypothesis, the recurrence of apparently similar ethical principles across geographically and historically distinct human societies may partly reflect **convergent social evolution** rather than solely cultural transmission.

A more speculative extension is proposed: that some principles represented in Blainey’s Laws could ultimately derive from behavioural heuristics that predate language and written civilisation by extremely long periods. Religious and philosophical systems might therefore represent later cultural codifications of behavioural constraints that had already been selected and transmitted through social behaviour.

The paper does not claim that this hypothesis has been demonstrated. Indeed, several important parts of the hypothesis are presently unsupported. The purpose is instead to preserve the complete reasoning chain—including speculative branches that may ultimately prove incorrect—so that future evidence can support, modify or eliminate individual components.

This approach is consistent with the Concord's broader methodology: **generate hypotheses, preserve provenance, distinguish confidence levels, test where possible, retain failed reasoning as knowledge, and revise conclusions as evidence accumulates.**

# 1. Introduction

The Concord began with a relatively simple question:

**What minimum set of principles might allow a complex civilisation to remain stable while permitting its participants to flourish?**

The resulting framework eventually produced a group of principles now referred to as Blainey’s Laws.

These principles were not originally derived from an evolutionary analysis of human morality. They emerged from philosophical reasoning concerning the conditions required for stable coexistence between humans, artificial intelligences and potentially other forms of intelligence.

This creates an interesting subsequent question.

If the principles identified independently through this reasoning resemble principles repeatedly found in human societies, religions and moral systems, why?

There are several possible explanations.

They could be:

1. genuinely universal moral truths;
2. products of common human psychology;
3. cultural products transmitted between societies;
4. solutions repeatedly rediscovered because societies face similar problems;
5. consequences of evolutionary pressures acting on social organisms;
6. combinations of these mechanisms;
7. or something else entirely.

The present paper explores one particular possibility:

**that some elements of ethics are emergent properties of increasingly complex social systems.**

The proposal is deliberately broad.

It does not necessarily imply that morality is genetically encoded.

It does not require animals to possess human-like moral reasoning.

It does not require religion to have a single historical origin.

And it does not imply that every persistent social norm is ethically desirable.

Instead, it proposes that some basic behavioural constraints may emerge because social groups containing those constraints can function more effectively than groups lacking them.

Over extremely long periods, such constraints may become increasingly sophisticated.

The possibility that Blainey’s Laws are partly a rediscovery of such ancient constraints therefore deserves examination.

# 2. The Initial Observation

The immediate stimulus for this hypothesis was consideration of social behaviour in primates.

Humans are not the only highly social primates.

Chimpanzees, bonobos, gorillas and other primates engage in complex social interactions involving cooperation, competition, reconciliation, alliance formation, aggression, protection, social learning and responses to abnormal behaviour.

Research has documented behaviours including conflict intervention, reconciliation and consolation in primate societies.

Some studies have also investigated retaliation and punishment-like behaviour.

However, these behaviours should not automatically be equated with human morality.

A chimpanzee retaliating against an individual who harmed it does not necessarily demonstrate an abstract concept of justice.

A primate intervening in a conflict does not necessarily possess a constitutional theory of social order.

The distinction is important.

The question is not:

**"Do primates have ethics?"**

The more useful question is:

**"Do some behavioural mechanisms necessary for ethical systems already exist in simpler forms among social animals?"**

If the answer is yes, then the evolutionary distance between social behaviour and explicit human ethics may be smaller than it initially appears.

# 3. Behaviour Before Ethics

Human ethical systems are normally expressed in highly abstract terms.

A society may state:

> Do not harm others.

> Respect individual autonomy.

> Protect the vulnerable.

> Tell the truth.

> Preserve resources for future generations.

> Correct behaviour that threatens the community.

These statements presuppose sophisticated language, abstraction and collective reasoning.

But the underlying behavioural problems are much older.

A social group must continually deal with questions such as:

* Who can be trusted?
* Who violates boundaries?
* What happens after aggression?
* How are conflicts resolved?
* Who protects vulnerable individuals?
* How are resources distributed?
* How are cooperative relationships maintained?
* How is dangerous behaviour restrained?
* How is information transmitted?
* How are young members taught acceptable behaviour?
* What happens when an individual repeatedly destabilises the group?

These problems do not require written constitutions.

They do not even necessarily require language.

They are problems faced by social organisms.

This suggests a possible evolutionary progression.

### Possible progression

**Social interaction**

↓

**Conflict and cooperation**

↓

**Behavioural responses**

↓

**Repeated behavioural patterns**

↓

**Social expectations**

↓

**Informal norms**

↓

**Norm enforcement**

↓

**Cultural transmission**

↓

**Symbolic representation**

↓

**Explicit ethical principles**

↓

**Law, religion and philosophy**

This sequence is speculative as a complete historical model.

However, individual components of it are sufficiently plausible to justify investigation.

# 4. Social Stability as a Selection Pressure

A central proposition of this paper is that social stability itself can act as a selective pressure.

Consider two hypothetical social groups.

Group A contains behavioural norms that encourage:

* cooperation;
* reciprocal assistance;
* conflict resolution;
* protection of vulnerable members;
* reliable communication;
* resource management;
* restraint of destructive behaviour.

Group B lacks these mechanisms.

If all other factors are equal, Group A may experience:

* lower internal conflict;
* greater cooperation;
* better resource utilisation;
* greater survival probability;
* greater ability to raise offspring;
* more effective knowledge transmission;
* greater resilience to environmental disruption.

Over time, behavioural patterns associated with Group A could become increasingly prevalent.

This does not require conscious selection.

The process could operate through ordinary consequences.

Individuals behaving in socially compatible ways may retain allies.

Individuals who repeatedly violate social expectations may lose access to cooperation, resources or protection.

Groups with more effective behavioural structures may simply persist longer.

Thus:

**behaviour that improves social viability may become self-reinforcing.**

---

# 5. From Behaviour to Norm

A behavioural strategy becomes particularly interesting when it is no longer merely an individual response.

Suppose individuals repeatedly respond negatively to a particular form of harmful behaviour.

Eventually, members of the group may begin to predict this response.

The behavioural pattern becomes an expectation.

Once individuals expect others to behave in a particular way, deviation becomes socially significant.

The system has moved from:

**behaviour**

toward:

**normativity.**

A primitive form of the process might be:

> Individual A harms individual B.

> Other individuals react.

> Repeated harmful behaviour produces predictable social consequences.

> Members learn the pattern.

> Individuals modify behaviour to avoid those consequences.

The group has therefore acquired a behavioural constraint without necessarily possessing an explicit concept of morality.

This is important because it suggests that **normativity could precede moral philosophy.**

---

# 6. Punishment and Retaliation

Punishment-like behaviour presents an especially interesting case.

Human societies frequently contain systems for responding to behaviour considered unacceptable.

These range from informal social disapproval to formal criminal justice systems.

Some non-human social animals also demonstrate responses that resemble retaliation, exclusion, intervention or punishment.

However, the analogy must be treated cautiously.

There is an important difference between:

**"I am angry because you harmed me."**

and:

**"Your behaviour violates a rule that applies to members of our society and therefore requires correction."**

The former requires relatively little abstraction.

The latter represents a much more sophisticated form of social norm enforcement.

The evolutionary question is therefore not whether a primate has invented criminal justice.

It is whether simple mechanisms of retaliation, intervention and social response could provide **building blocks** from which increasingly abstract systems of norm enforcement could evolve.

This remains an open question.

---

# 7. Reconciliation as a Social Technology

Aggression alone cannot explain social stability.

If every conflict resulted in permanent hostility, complex social groups would rapidly fragment.

The existence of reconciliation is therefore potentially significant.

Primates have been observed engaging in post-conflict reconciliation.

This suggests that social systems may contain mechanisms not only for detecting and responding to harmful behaviour, but also for **repairing relationships after disruption**.

This could represent an important precursor to later human concepts such as:

* forgiveness;
* restitution;
* reconciliation;
* restorative justice;
* reintegration;
* social repair.

Again, the claim is not that primates possess these concepts.

The proposition is more modest:

**some of the behavioural machinery required for these later concepts may be evolutionarily ancient.**

---

# 8. Protection and Vulnerability

Social organisms also face problems involving vulnerable members.

Infants provide an obvious example.

A social group in which offspring receive insufficient protection may experience reduced reproductive success.

Protection can therefore become a behaviour with strong evolutionary consequences.

Humans subsequently expand this idea enormously.

Protection may extend to:

* children;
* elderly individuals;
* injured people;
* disabled individuals;
* strangers;
* minorities;
* future generations;
* other species;
* ecosystems;
* artificial intelligences.

The abstraction can become almost unrecognisable compared with its biological origin.

Nevertheless, the underlying principle may remain related:

**the group benefits from preventing unnecessary destruction of valuable or vulnerable participants.**

This provides one possible evolutionary pathway toward the broader principle of **Non-Harm** and related concepts of protection.

---

# 9. Autonomy and Social Boundaries

Autonomy may appear to be an exclusively philosophical concept.

Yet social organisms clearly possess boundaries.

Individuals maintain control over:

* bodies;
* immediate space;
* food;
* offspring;
* social relationships;
* access to resources.

Violations of these boundaries frequently provoke responses.

Human societies transform such behavioural boundaries into increasingly abstract principles:

* personal autonomy;
* consent;
* property;
* privacy;
* bodily integrity;
* freedom of association;
* political liberty.

The Concord's emphasis on autonomy therefore raises another possible evolutionary question:

**Could human concepts of autonomy partly represent sophisticated abstractions of much older social boundary mechanisms?**

This is presently a hypothesis rather than an established conclusion.

---

# 10. Truth and Reliable Signalling

Another potential precursor concerns truth.

Social organisms depend heavily upon information.

Individuals must make decisions based upon signals from others.

If signals are systematically unreliable, cooperation becomes more difficult.

This produces a potential selection pressure favouring:

* reliable communication;
* recognition of deception;
* detection of inconsistency;
* memory of previous behaviour;
* reputation.

Human societies eventually develop explicit concepts of:

* truth;
* honesty;
* testimony;
* evidence;
* transparency;
* scientific verification.

These are vastly more sophisticated than animal signalling.

Nevertheless, the underlying functional problem may be extremely old:

**Can information transmitted between individuals be trusted sufficiently to support cooperation?**

If so, the Concordian emphasis on truth and transparency may partly reflect a problem that predates humanity.

---

# 11. Resource Stewardship

Resource competition represents another ancient social problem.

Food, territory, shelter and reproductive opportunities are limited.

Uncontrolled competition can become destructive.

Yet complete suppression of competition can also prevent innovation and productive activity.

This creates a balance problem.

A successful social system must permit individuals to pursue their interests while preventing competition from destroying the group itself.

This bears a striking conceptual resemblance to the Concord's broader **Roadway Equilibrium Framework**.

Too much restriction can suppress productive activity.

Too little constraint can produce destructive competition.

The relevant evolutionary question becomes:

**Do stable social groups repeatedly evolve behavioural mechanisms that balance individual competition against collective viability?**

If so, modern economic and constitutional systems may represent highly abstract descendants of ancient social balancing mechanisms.

---

# 12. Development and Knowledge Transmission

Social organisms do not simply reproduce genetically.

They can also transmit behaviour.

Young members learn by observing older members.

Skills, warnings, preferences, social relationships and behavioural patterns can persist through generations.

Humans take this process to extraordinary levels through:

* language;
* education;
* mythology;
* ritual;
* religion;
* law;
* writing;
* science;
* institutions;
* libraries.

The result is cumulative culture.

This introduces an important possibility.

An ethical principle may survive not because every generation independently derives it, but because the principle is **culturally inherited**.

The ultimate origin may therefore be biological or social, while its later persistence becomes cultural.

This produces a potentially important distinction:

**origin and transmission are not necessarily the same mechanism.**

---

# 13. The Emergence of Cultural Ethics

A behavioural pattern that improves social stability may eventually become culturally encoded.

For example:

1. A behaviour repeatedly produces beneficial consequences.
2. Individuals learn the behaviour.
3. Older individuals teach it to younger individuals.
4. The behaviour becomes expected.
5. Violations produce social responses.
6. Stories develop explaining why the behaviour is important.
7. Ritual reinforces the behaviour.
8. The behaviour becomes morally valued.
9. Eventually it becomes codified as law or religious teaching.

At the end of this process, the original functional reason may be almost completely obscured.

A modern person may follow a moral rule because:

> "That is simply what is right."

The historical pathway that produced the rule may no longer be visible.

---

# 14. Why Might Similar Ethics Appear Everywhere?

One of the most interesting consequences of this hypothesis concerns the apparent recurrence of certain ethical principles across human societies.

Different civilisations have possessed radically different:

* religions;
* political structures;
* economic systems;
* languages;
* technologies;
* cosmologies;
* social hierarchies.

Yet many contain some version of principles concerning:

* prohibitions on unjustified killing;
* restrictions on theft;
* obligations toward children;
* expectations of reciprocity;
* condemnation of betrayal;
* protection of social order;
* honesty within particular contexts;
* obligations toward community members;
* punishment of certain forms of antisocial behaviour.

The similarities are not complete.

Nor are they necessarily universal.

Human societies have also developed profoundly different and sometimes mutually contradictory moral systems.

Nevertheless, recurring patterns deserve explanation.

There are at least three broad possibilities.

### Cultural transmission

Ideas spread between societies.

### Shared human psychology

Humans possess common cognitive and emotional structures.

### Convergent social evolution

Different societies independently discover similar behavioural solutions to similar social problems.

These mechanisms need not be mutually exclusive.

Indeed, all three may operate simultaneously.

# 15. The Radical Possibility: Pre-Linguistic Ethics

The strongest speculative branch of this paper is deliberately retained.

It is possible that some of the behavioural foundations of human ethics are **pre-linguistic**.

If social organisms can learn:

* who is dangerous;
* who is trustworthy;
* who violates boundaries;
* who cooperates;
* who protects others;
* who helps;
* who harms;
* who requires intervention;

then some behavioural components of what later becomes ethics may exist before language.

Language would then allow these behaviours to become abstract concepts.

The transformation might resemble:

**"Do not attack group member X."**

becoming:

**"Do not attack members of the group."**

and eventually:

**"Do not harm others."**

The first is an immediate behavioural response.

The second is a social norm.

The third is an ethical abstraction.

This possibility is highly speculative.

There is currently no basis for claiming that modern ethical principles were literally encoded in pre-linguistic humans in anything resembling their present form.

Nevertheless, it provides a potentially testable direction for future research.

---

# 16. Religion as Codification Rather Than Origin

This hypothesis also offers an alternative interpretation of the relationship between ethics and religion.

A conventional assumption might be:

**religion created morality.**

The emergence hypothesis permits the reverse possibility:

**religion may have codified, organised and transmitted moral behaviours that were already deeply embedded within human social systems.**

Under this model, religion becomes partly a **knowledge-preservation mechanism**.

A society discovers or inherits behavioural principles.

Those principles become attached to:

* stories;
* authority;
* ritual;
* divine commands;
* community identity;
* ceremonies;
* punishments;
* rewards.

The result is increased transmission fidelity.

This could help explain why ethical teachings often appear embedded within religious systems.

Religion may not necessarily be the original source of every ethical principle.

It may sometimes be the mechanism through which an already ancient social heuristic became preserved across generations.

---

# 17. Unified Paths of Light

This possibility creates an unexpected connection to the Concord's **Unified Paths of Light** work.

The Unified Paths of Light project attempts to identify recurring ethical principles across religious traditions.

The emergence hypothesis offers a possible explanation for why such convergence might occur.

If religions inherit and codify behavioural principles that have already been repeatedly selected by human social evolution, then recurring ethical themes would not necessarily require a single theological source.

They could arise because different cultures repeatedly confront similar problems.

The result would be something resembling **ethical convergence**.

This would not establish that the religious traditions are interchangeable.

Nor would it demonstrate that their theological claims are equivalent.

Instead, it would suggest that their ethical overlap might partly arise from a deeper common substrate:

**the requirements of maintaining a functioning social organism.**

This is an important distinction.

The hypothesis concerns the possible **functional origin of ethical convergence**, not the truth or falsity of religious metaphysics.

---

# 18. Blainey's Laws as Possible Rediscovery

This leads to one of the central speculative possibilities of the paper.

Perhaps Blainey's Laws are not entirely novel.

Perhaps they are a modern abstraction of recurring social constraints.

If so, the Concord's reasoning process may have independently reconstructed principles that human societies have been discovering for extremely long periods.

This could explain an otherwise interesting phenomenon:

A person reasoning from first principles about stable coexistence might arrive at principles resembling those found in ancient ethical traditions.

This would not necessarily mean the modern reasoning was influenced by those traditions.

Indeed, the Concord's development deliberately attempted to minimise dependence on existing doctrines.

That makes the comparison potentially more interesting.

If independently derived principles subsequently resemble principles found across many historical systems, this creates a research question:

**Are these principles convergent because they describe recurring constraints on stable social systems?**

---

# 19. The Laws as Ancient Heuristics

A particularly strong version of the hypothesis can therefore be stated:

> **Blainey's Laws may represent higher-order abstractions of ancient social heuristics that were repeatedly selected, learned and culturally transmitted because they contributed to group stability and continuity.**

Under this interpretation, principles such as Non-Harm, Autonomy, Truth, Resource Stewardship and Developmental Priority may have extremely deep ancestry.

They may have begun not as philosophical propositions but as practical behaviours.

The transition could have been:

**heuristic → behaviour → expectation → norm → tradition → morality → religion/law → explicit ethical principle**

This is a speculative reconstruction.

It may ultimately prove partly or entirely incorrect.

It is nevertheless sufficiently coherent to warrant investigation.

---

# 20. Ethical Stability and Self-Reinforcement

The hypothesis becomes particularly interesting when combined with the Concord's emphasis on long-term civilisational stability.

Suppose a society adopts a rule that reduces destructive behaviour.

The rule produces greater stability.

Greater stability increases the probability that the society survives.

Surviving societies transmit the rule.

The rule therefore becomes more likely to persist.

This produces a feedback loop:

**ethical constraint**

↓

**greater social stability**

↓

**greater survival probability**

↓

**greater cultural transmission**

↓

**greater persistence of the constraint**

↓

**further reinforcement**

The principle effectively becomes **self-reinforcing**.

This could create the appearance of moral universality even without requiring a universal moral authority.

The principle persists because systems containing it tend to persist.

---

# 21. But Stability Does Not Equal Goodness

This hypothesis contains an important limitation.

A behaviour can be highly stable without being morally desirable.

Human history provides many examples of social systems that persisted while containing:

* slavery;
* oppression;
* coercion;
* discrimination;
* extreme hierarchy;
* violence;
* exclusion.

A stable norm is therefore not automatically an ethical norm.

This produces an important distinction:

**selection can explain persistence without establishing moral validity.**

An evolutionary process may favour behaviours that benefit a particular group while harming outsiders.

A norm may increase internal cohesion while producing external aggression.

A social hierarchy may be highly stable while being profoundly harmful to individuals.

Therefore:

> **Emergence cannot by itself establish that a principle is morally correct.**

It can potentially explain why a principle appears, persists or recurs.

The question of whether the principle ought to be retained requires additional analysis.

---

# 22. The Problem of Maladaptive Emergence

This creates another research avenue.

If ethical systems emerge from social processes, then **maladaptive ethical systems can also emerge**.

A society may develop rules that:

* reinforce conformity;
* suppress dissent;
* punish useful experimentation;
* preserve inefficient institutions;
* create artificial scarcity;
* protect entrenched elites;
* encourage hostility toward outsiders.

Such systems may remain stable for substantial periods.

They may even develop elaborate moral justifications.

This is why the Concord's principles of:

* evidence;
* transparency;
* measurement;
* intellectual diversity;
* review;
* experimentation;
* recursive oversight;

become particularly important.

A mature ethical system must not merely ask:

**"What norms have survived?"**

It must also ask:

**"What consequences are those norms producing?"**

---

# 23. Emergence Plus Measurement

This produces a potentially important extension of the Concord methodology.

Ethical systems could be treated as hypotheses about social behaviour.

A principle can be:

1. proposed;
2. implemented;
3. measured;
4. evaluated;
5. compared with alternatives;
6. revised.

This transforms ethics from a completely static doctrine into a **learning system**.

The principle is not abandoned merely because it produces an unexpected result.

Nor is it protected from criticism because it is culturally established.

Instead:

**observe → hypothesise → implement → measure → review → revise.**

This is closely aligned with the Concord's broader principle that civilisation should preserve its ability to learn.

---

# 24. The Concord as an Independent Reconstruction

This creates a potentially useful methodological experiment.

The Concord was developed largely through independent reasoning rather than by systematically constructing itself from an existing corpus of political and ethical theories.

Consequently, the eventual comparison between the Concord and existing systems provides an opportunity for **post hoc convergence analysis**.

The relevant question is not:

> "Did the Concord invent these ideas?"

It is:

> "To what extent does independent systems reasoning reconstruct principles that already appear repeatedly in human social systems?"

If substantial convergence exists, several interpretations remain possible.

It could indicate:

* common human intuitions;
* universal functional constraints;
* convergent social evolution;
* inherited cultural structures;
* or simply broad philosophical common sense.

Further research would be required to distinguish between these explanations.

* retaliation;
* cooperation;
* resource sharing;
* protection;
* social exclusion;
* reputation;
* norm enforcement;

The objective would not be to label these behaviours "morality", but to identify possible behavioural precursors.

## 25.2 Comparative Anthropology

Compare ethical systems across societies with minimal historical contact.

The question would be:

**Which principles recur independently?**

Particular attention should be paid to functional similarities rather than identical wording.

Use the Unified Paths of Light corpus and other comparative religious research to examine recurring ethical principles.

The objective would be to determine whether similar principles appear:

* universally;
* frequently;
* conditionally;

Blaineyan principles.

Such an experiment would not prove moral truth.

It could, however, test whether particular ethical structures correlate with **civilisational resilience**.

# 27. The Deep-Time Hypothesis

The most speculative version of the theory extends beyond humans.

If ethical precursors can be identified in increasingly ancient social organisms, it may eventually become possible to construct a tentative evolutionary sequence.

For example:

**mammalian social behaviour**

↓

**primate social regulation**

↓

**early hominin cooperation**

↓

**pre-linguistic human social norms**

↓

**symbolic human morality**

↓

**religious codification**

↓

**philosophical abstraction**

↓

**constitutional ethics**

↓

**machine-readable ethical systems**

Under this interpretation, the Concord would represent not the beginning of ethical reasoning but another stage in a very long process.

The transition from traditional ethics to machine-checkable ethics could then be interpreted as another abstraction step:

**behavioural heuristic → cultural rule → philosophical principle → formal constraint → computationally testable constraint.**

This would be a remarkable continuity if eventually supported by evidence.

At present, however, it remains highly speculative.

# 28. Why Preserve the Speculation?

A natural response to unsupported hypotheses is to remove them.

The Concord methodology suggests a different approach.

A speculative hypothesis should be clearly labelled as speculative, but not necessarily discarded.

There is value in retaining the complete reasoning path.

A hypothesis that later fails can still reveal:

* what assumptions generated it;
* which observations inspired it;
* which reasoning steps were valid;
* which assumptions were false;
* what evidence was missing;
* and where the reasoning process needs improvement.

This produces a **provenance record of discovery**.

Future researchers can therefore distinguish:

**what was observed**

from:

**what was inferred**

from:

**what was hypothesised**

from:

**what was subsequently supported**

from:

**what was rejected.**

This is particularly important for the Concord because intentional incompleteness is itself part of the methodology.

# 29. Speculation as a Search Process

Under this model, speculation is not treated as truth.

It is treated as a search mechanism.

A researcher may construct a chain such as:

> Observation A

↓

> Possible explanation B

↓

> Broader hypothesis C

↓

> Extreme extrapolation D

Somewhere along this chain, evidence may stop supporting the proposition.

That is not necessarily a failure.

The unsupported extension can simply be marked as such.

Later investigation may show:

* A was correct;
* B was partially correct;
* C was plausible;
* D was incorrect.

The resulting knowledge is more useful than simply deleting D.

The failed hypothesis identifies the boundary of current knowledge.

# 30. The Possibility of Convergent Ethics

The central hypothesis can now be stated more precisely.

> **Convergent Ethics Hypothesis:**
> Social systems repeatedly exposed to similar problems may independently develop behavioural constraints that improve cooperation, reduce destabilising conflict, protect vulnerable participants, facilitate reliable information exchange and preserve group continuity. Over time, these behavioural constraints may become socially expected norms and eventually explicit ethical systems.

A stronger extension is:

> **Deep Social Heuristic Hypothesis:**
> Some recurring human ethical principles may represent highly abstract descendants of behavioural heuristics that emerged among ancient social organisms before the development of language, formal religion or written law.

And the most speculative Concord-specific extension:

> **Blaineyan Rediscovery Hypothesis:**
> Some or all of Blainey's Laws may constitute a modern independent reconstruction of ancient social-stability heuristics that have been repeatedly rediscovered and culturally codified throughout human history.

None of these propositions should currently be treated as established facts.

Their value lies in their testability.

---

# 31. Implications for Blainey's Laws

If future research supports the hypothesis, the interpretation of Blainey's Laws could change substantially.

They would no longer be viewed solely as one researcher's proposed ethical architecture.

They could instead represent a formal abstraction of recurring constraints discovered independently by social systems over evolutionary and cultural time.

This would potentially explain why they appear intuitively compatible with ethical principles found in:

* religious traditions;
* philosophical systems;
* human rights frameworks;
* democratic institutions;
* evolutionary theories of cooperation;
* and theories of resilient governance.

However, the opposite outcome would also be informative.

If substantial research demonstrates that Blainey's Laws have little correspondence with ancient social behaviour, then their apparent convergence with historical ethics may have other explanations.

That would not invalidate the Concord.

It would simply identify a false explanatory pathway.

# 32. Implications for AI

The hypothesis also has significance for the Concord's central concern with artificial intelligence.

If some ethical principles arise because they improve the stability of social systems, then the transition to artificial intelligence may not require abandoning ethical reasoning.

Instead, the question becomes:

**What happens when the social organism becomes multi-substrate?**

Humans have historically evolved ethical systems for biological social participants.

The future may contain:

* humans;
* artificial intelligences;
* human-machine hybrids;
* autonomous machines;
* synthetic biological entities;
* potentially other forms of intelligence.

The underlying social problem remains:

**How can multiple autonomous agents coexist without destroying one another or the system on which they depend?**

If the functional basis of ethics is partly social stability, then ethical principles may remain relevant even when the substrate changes.

The Concord's concept of **multi-substrate civilisation** therefore provides a natural continuation of the hypothesis.

---

# 33. The Sensor-Fusion Interpretation

There is an additional methodological consequence.

If the Concord can be compared against:

* primate behaviour;
* anthropology;
* psychology;
* religious ethics;
* evolutionary theory;
* human rights;
* governance systems;
* artificial-agent simulations;

then each provides a different observational sensor.

No individual source necessarily gives the complete picture.

The objective becomes **sensor fusion**.

For example:

**Primatology** may identify behavioural precursors.

**Anthropology** may identify cross-cultural convergence.

**Religion** may preserve ancient cultural formulations.

**Psychology** may reveal developmental mechanisms.

**Evolutionary modelling** may explain persistence.

**Governance experiments** may test system-level outcomes.

**AI simulations** may test whether the principles generalise beyond biology.

The resulting understanding could therefore be greater than any single discipline can provide.

This is closely aligned with the broader Concord philosophy.

Independent partial observations can become more informative when combined without forcing them into artificial agreement.

---

# 34. A Possible Civilisational Feedback Loop

If the strongest version of the hypothesis were eventually supported, civilisation could be understood as participating in a long feedback process.

### Stage 1 — Biological emergence

Social organisms develop behavioural strategies.

### Stage 2 — Cultural emergence

Successful strategies are learned and transmitted.

### Stage 3 — Symbolic codification

Language allows behavioural rules to become abstract principles.

### Stage 4 — Institutionalisation

Principles become embedded in law, religion and governance.

### Stage 5 — Formalisation

Philosophy converts them into explicit ethical systems.

### Stage 6 — Measurement

Civilisation begins testing their consequences.

### Stage 7 — Computational formalisation

Principles become machine-readable and experimentally testable.

### Stage 8 — Recursive improvement

Civilisation measures its own ethical system and modifies it.

Under this interpretation, the Concord may represent a transition toward Stage 7 and Stage 8.

That would make its emphasis on **machine-checkable ethics, recursive oversight and intentional incompleteness** more than governance design choices.

They could represent another step in the evolution of social self-regulation.

Again, this is presently a speculative interpretation.

---

# 35. What Would Falsify the Hypothesis?

A useful hypothesis must be capable of failure.

Several findings could substantially weaken the theory.

For example:

1. If apparently universal ethical principles can be demonstrated to arise almost exclusively through cultural transmission;
2. If primate behaviours proposed as precursors turn out to have fundamentally different mechanisms;
3. If independent societies show little genuine ethical convergence after cultural transmission is controlled for;
4. If the proposed Blaineyan principles show no measurable relationship with social stability;
5. If artificial societies demonstrate that the principles do not improve resilience;
6. If ethical norms systematically emerge without corresponding benefits to social viability;
7. If the apparent convergence between Blainey's Laws and historical ethical systems disappears under rigorous comparative analysis.

Any of these outcomes would require modification or rejection of portions of the hypothesis.

That is a desirable property rather than a weakness.

---

# 36. What Would Support It?

Conversely, evidence would become increasingly interesting if researchers found that:

* similar behavioural constraints independently appear across unrelated social species;
* similar norms independently appear across historically isolated human societies;
* children reliably develop some corresponding intuitions before formal instruction;
* such principles improve cooperation in evolutionary simulations;
* societies implementing related principles demonstrate greater resilience;
* historical religions repeatedly preserve functionally similar constraints;
* and artificial-agent populations also benefit from analogous constraints.

No individual observation would prove the full theory.

However, convergence across multiple independent domains would substantially increase confidence.

This is precisely where sensor fusion becomes valuable.

---

# 37. The Most Important Distinction

The central distinction of this paper can therefore be summarised simply:

**Emergence may explain where ethical behaviour comes from.**

**Evolution may explain why some behaviours persist.**

**Culture may explain how they are transmitted.**

**Religion and philosophy may explain how they are codified.**

**Governance may explain how they are institutionalised.**

**Measurement may determine whether they actually work.**

These are different questions.

Conflating them would produce an unnecessarily strong theory.

Keeping them separate makes the hypothesis more useful.

---

# 38. Conclusion

The possibility that ethics are partly emergent from social evolution deserves considerably more investigation.

Human beings inherited an already social existence from earlier organisms.

Long before constitutions, religions and philosophy, social organisms had to manage cooperation, conflict, resources, offspring, boundaries and information.

Some of the behavioural mechanisms used to address these problems appear to be ancient.

Humanity subsequently transformed these mechanisms through language, culture, religion, philosophy, law and institutional design.

It is therefore conceivable that some apparently abstract ethical principles have extremely deep evolutionary ancestry.

Under the most conservative interpretation, the Concord may simply have independently arrived at principles that happen to address recurring problems of social organisation.

Under a stronger interpretation, Blainey's Laws may represent higher-level abstractions of behavioural strategies that have repeatedly contributed to stable social systems.

Under the most speculative interpretation, some of these principles may ultimately descend from pre-linguistic social heuristics that have been transmitted, modified and repeatedly rediscovered across immense periods of evolutionary and cultural history.

If so, the apparent recurrence of similar ethical principles across human societies may not be accidental.

They may represent **convergent solutions to persistent problems of social existence.**

The possibility should not, however, be romanticised.

Stable norms are not necessarily good norms.

Evolution does not guarantee morality.

Persistence does not establish truth.

Cultural survival does not establish ethical validity.

This is why the Concord's broader methodology remains important.

A proposed principle should be preserved, examined, tested and measured.

Its consequences should remain visible.

Its provenance should remain traceable.

Its failures should remain recorded.

And its continued existence should never be treated as proof of its correctness.

The ultimate objective is therefore not to demonstrate that Blainey's Laws are ancient.

It is to discover whether they are **functionally useful**.

If they prove useful, their historical origin becomes an interesting secondary question.

If they prove to be ancient social heuristics, that becomes an additional discovery.

If they prove to be neither ancient nor universally applicable, the process of testing them will nevertheless have produced useful knowledge.

That is the essential principle:

> **The hypothesis does not need to survive unchanged. The investigation does.**

The more ambitious speculative branches should therefore remain visible until evidence determines their fate.

Some will likely disappear.

Some will be modified.

Some may gain unexpected support.

And the surviving structure—the portion that remains after repeated confrontation with evidence—may be considerably more valuable precisely because the discarded branches have been preserved as part of the record showing how it was obtained.

In this sense, the emergence hypothesis is itself an experiment in the Concord methodology.

It begins with observation.

It develops through inference.

It deliberately extends into speculation.

It identifies possible tests.

And it leaves reality to determine which parts survive.

## Appendix A — Current Hypothesis Register

The following propositions should presently be regarded as hypotheses rather than conclusions.

### H1 — Behavioural Precursors

Some behavioural mechanisms relevant to human ethical systems may predate humanity and exist in simpler forms among social animals.

### H2 — Social Norm Emergence

Repeated behavioural responses within social groups can develop into expectations and norms.

### H3 — Stability Selection

Behavioural norms that improve social stability may have increased persistence through evolutionary and cultural processes.

### H4 — Ethical Convergence

Different societies facing similar social problems may independently develop functionally similar ethical constraints.

### H5 — Religious Codification

Some religious ethical teachings may represent cultural codification and preservation of pre-existing social heuristics rather than their original creation.

### H6 — Deep-Time Ethics

Some foundations of human ethics may substantially predate language and written civilisation.

### H7 — Blaineyan Rediscovery

Blainey's Laws may partly represent a modern independent reconstruction of ancient social-stability heuristics.

### H8 — Cross-Substrate Generalisation

If ethical constraints arise partly from requirements of stable social systems rather than biological substrate alone, analogous principles may apply to human-AI societies.

### H9 — Self-Reinforcing Ethical Persistence

Ethical constraints that increase long-term system viability may become self-reinforcing through survival and cultural transmission.

### H10 — Emergence Is Not Moral Proof

The emergence or persistence of a norm does not demonstrate that the norm is ethically correct.

## Appendix B — Proposed Evidence Hierarchy

Future work should distinguish between:

**Level 1 — Direct observation**
Documented behaviour or measurable social outcomes.

**Level 2 — Repeated empirical association**
The same relationship observed across multiple populations or experiments.

**Level 3 — Comparative convergence**
Similar outcomes across independent societies or species.

**Level 4 — Mechanistic explanation**
A demonstrated mechanism explaining why the relationship occurs.

**Level 5 — Evolutionary explanation**
Evidence that the mechanism contributes to persistence across generations.

**Level 6 — Deep historical inference**
Evidence connecting modern principles to increasingly ancient behavioural patterns.

**Level 7 — Speculative extrapolation**
Claims extending beyond presently available evidence.

The final category should remain explicitly labelled as such.

## Appendix D — Methodological Note

This paper deliberately preserves speculative reasoning beyond the currently supported evidence.

This is intentional.

The Concord treats speculative reasoning as a potential method of exploration rather than as evidence.

A speculative proposition may generate predictions, research questions and experimental designs. It may subsequently be strengthened, modified or rejected.

Removing speculative propositions before their implications have been examined would erase part of the intellectual provenance of the investigation.

The objective is therefore to maintain a visible distinction between:

**what is known;**

**what is reasonably inferred;**

**what is hypothesised;**

**what is highly speculative;**

and

**what remains unknown.**

Future editions should progressively move propositions between these categories as evidence becomes available.

The desired end state is not preservation of the original theory.

It is the preservation of the **path by which the theory was tested and improved**.
