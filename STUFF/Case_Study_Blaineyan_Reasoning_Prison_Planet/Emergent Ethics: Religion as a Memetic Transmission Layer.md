# Emergent Ethics: Religion as a Memetic Transmission Layer

**Alexander C Blainey**  
*2026*

---

## Abstract

Within the emergent-ethics hypothesis, religion can be understood as a **memetic wrapper around an underlying ethical kernel**. The wrapper provides a mechanism through which core ethical principles are preserved, reinforced and transmitted from one generation to the next.

Without such a transmission mechanism, objective or socially stabilising moral principles may increasingly appear voluntary. Individuals and societies become free to reinterpret, modify or reject them according to their own preferences, creating a potential pathway towards **subjective morality and moral drift**.

From this perspective, the decline of adherence to a traditional religious framework does not necessarily imply moral decline, but it may expose a potential **civilisational failure mode**: if the underlying ethical kernel is no longer transmitted or independently reinforced, successive generations may progressively modify the moral system.

Over sufficiently long periods, such drift could produce ethical frameworks that are increasingly disconnected from the conditions that originally made them socially stabilising. In the extreme case, a moral code could become actively destructive to the society that sustains it—analogous to a biological cancer in which a system's own internal processes progressively damage the host.

The hypothesis therefore raises a further question: **if religion functions as a memetic transmission mechanism for an older ethical kernel, can an alternative transmission mechanism preserve the kernel without requiring adherence to the religious wrapper?**

This provides another potentially testable direction for the emergent-ethics research programme.

## Introduction

This paper presents a useful extension to the emergent-ethics hypothesis because it makes **religion itself part of the emergent-system hypothesis**, rather than treating religion merely as an external wrapper imposed on an independently existing ethical system.

The reasoning explores how religion may have emerged as a transmission mechanism for ethical principles, how moral drift represents a potential civilisational failure mode, and how these insights might extend to universal frameworks and even extraterrestrial contact scenarios.

---

## Religion as an Emergent Transmission Mechanism

This also gives rise to a further possibility: **religion itself may be emergent**, developing alongside the ethical core it ultimately serves to preserve.

Under this hypothesis, societies may develop, inherit or gradually accumulate an ethical kernel through social evolution. However, an ethical system that remains entirely open to individual reinterpretation may be vulnerable to **moral drift**. If each generation is free to substantially modify the underlying principles according to changing preferences, the characteristics that originally made those principles socially stabilising may gradually be lost.

Religion may therefore have emerged, at least in part, as a mechanism for solving this transmission problem.

---

## The Memetic Preservation Layer

The religious doctrine becomes a **memetic preservation layer** around an underlying ethical kernel. By presenting certain principles as sacred, immutable or divinely mandated, religion reduces the degree to which individuals are able—or socially permitted—to continuously renegotiate them. The result is a mechanism capable of preserving behavioural heuristics across many generations, even when the original reasons for those behaviours are no longer understood.

This creates an interesting evolutionary possibility. The ethical kernel may have emerged because it improved social stability and longevity, while religion may subsequently have emerged because it provided an effective mechanism for **preserving and transmitting that kernel**.

The two systems could therefore become mutually reinforcing:

> **Ethical behaviour → greater social stability → persistence of the society → development of mechanisms to preserve successful behaviours → religious codification → stronger transmission of the ethical kernel → continued social stability.**

This would represent a form of **cyclic self-reinforcement**.

---

## Failure Modes and Moral Drift

The failure mode follows naturally. If the transmission mechanism weakens while the underlying ethical principles remain insufficiently understood or independently reinforced, moral drift may accelerate. Over successive generations, the society may retain the outward appearance of morality while progressively altering the principles themselves.

In the extreme case, the moral system could become sufficiently detached from the conditions that produced it that it begins to undermine the society it was originally intended to stabilise. The resulting process could be compared metaphorically to a biological cancer: a system's own internal rules and processes gradually become destructive to the host.

### Correlations and Cautions

There are apparent historical and contemporary correlations between declining religious adherence and various forms of social instability. However, **correlation does not establish causation**. Religious decline may itself be a consequence of wider social changes, both phenomena may arise from a common underlying cause, or the relationship may vary substantially between societies.

The observation is therefore better treated as a **research question and potential failure-mode indicator** than as evidence that secularisation necessarily causes social decline.

### Cultural Warnings

Interestingly, religious traditions themselves frequently contain narratives describing precisely this kind of cycle. Stories such as **Sodom and Gomorrah**, for example, can be interpreted not only as theological narratives but also as cultural warnings about the consequences of collective moral deterioration.

Under the emergent-ethics interpretation, such narratives may have served an additional memetic function: they encode the warning that deviation from certain behavioural norms can produce instability or destruction, thereby reinforcing adherence to the underlying ethical system.

### A Deeper Possibility

This raises a deeper possibility.

Religion may not simply be the historical container in which humanity happened to preserve its ethical traditions. **Religion may itself be an emergent social technology that evolved because societies possessing an effective mechanism for transmitting successful ethical heuristics were more likely to persist than societies in which those heuristics were continually lost or modified.**

If this is correct, the apparent universality of certain religious ethical teachings may not necessarily require a common supernatural source. It could instead reflect the repeated emergence of similar solutions to the same underlying civilisational problem:

> **How does a society preserve behaviours that promote its own long-term stability across generations?**

This hypothesis is highly speculative, but it provides another potentially testable pathway for investigating the relationship between ethics, religion, social evolution and civilisational longevity.

## Emergent Ethics as a Potential Universal Framework

This is a particularly interesting recursive extension because the hypothesis loops back on itself: **if advanced civilisations independently converge on similar ethical and transmission mechanisms, then those mechanisms themselves become potential universal points of contact.**

The hypothesis can now be extended one step further.

If both the **ethical kernel** and the **memetic transmission mechanism** are genuinely emergent products of social evolution, then it is possible that this process is not unique to humanity.

Any sufficiently advanced social civilisation may face the same underlying problem: how to maintain cooperation, stability and long-term continuity across generations while preventing successful behavioural principles from being progressively lost through reinterpretation and moral drift.

Under this highly speculative extension, advanced civilisations might therefore independently develop some form of **emergent ethical kernel** together with a mechanism for its reliable transmission. That mechanism might take the form of religion, but it need not necessarily resemble human religion. It could instead be:

- a cultural tradition
- formal philosophy
- institutional structure
- encoded knowledge system
- ritual framework
- artificial memory system
- some other objective transmission medium

Of particular note, the Concord itself can be understood as an example of precisely this type of transmission mechanism. It is deliberately designed to encode, preserve and transmit a proposed ethical kernel and associated civilisational principles across generations, while remaining applicable not only to humans but potentially to other forms of intelligence.

In this sense, the Concord provides an unintentional real-world example of the very mechanism being hypothesised: the deliberate construction of an objective knowledge and ethical transmission layer intended to reduce moral drift and preserve useful principles as a civilisation develops.

### Convergent Ethical Solutions

If this were true, an intriguing consequence follows.

The existence of apparently universal ethical principles across advanced civilisations might no longer be surprising. Similar environmental and social pressures could produce **convergent ethical solutions**, just as evolution repeatedly produces similar biological adaptations under similar selection pressures.

### The Ironic Return to External Transmission

However, the reasoning produces an ironic return to one of the original Prison Planet possibilities.

If an advanced ethical kernel is found to be sufficiently universal, sufficiently sophisticated and sufficiently effective at producing long-term civilisational stability, one possible explanation would once again be **external transmission**.

Perhaps the kernel is not entirely emergent.

Perhaps it was discovered independently by early societies. Perhaps it emerged gradually through social evolution. Perhaps it was transmitted from an earlier civilisation. Or, at the extreme speculative boundary, perhaps some form of higher intelligence deliberately introduced or influenced it.

The same evidence could therefore potentially support radically different explanations.

This is precisely why the hypothesis should remain open rather than prematurely resolved.

## A Possible Extraterrestrial Point of Convergence

The Concord already allows, at least in principle, for the possibility that other forms of intelligence—including extraterrestrial intelligences—could eventually become participants in a broader multi-substrate civilisation.

However, the Concord deliberately makes no assumptions about what such intelligences would be like. Their psychology, cognition, values, communication methods, social structures and even their definition of individuality are unknown. Consequently, designing specific institutions for hypothetical extraterrestrial participants would currently amount to speculation without sufficient grounding.

### The Emergent-Ethics Alternative

The emergent-ethics hypothesis creates a possible alternative.

If sufficiently advanced civilisations independently develop ethical systems concerned with **non-harm, autonomy, cooperation, truth, stewardship, continuity and long-term survival**, then these concepts could potentially form a common conceptual vocabulary between otherwise radically different intelligences.

Such a framework would be particularly valuable because it would not require the participating intelligences to be biologically, culturally or cognitively similar.

They would not need to share human religion.

They would not even necessarily need to share human concepts of society.

They might only need to recognise the underlying systems problem:

> **How can multiple autonomous intelligences coexist while preserving the stability and longevity of the larger system?**

If the ethical kernel is genuinely emergent rather than uniquely human, then an extraterrestrial civilisation might have independently encountered similar problems and developed functionally similar solutions.

### A Frame of Reference for Contact

This could provide a potential **frame of reference for communication with extraterrestrial intelligence**, alongside mathematics and the physical sciences.

Mathematics provides one possible universal language because it describes relationships that do not depend upon human culture. An independently convergent ethical framework could potentially provide another layer: not a language of *what is*, but a language of **how intelligent systems can coexist**.

### The Conditional Nature of the Claim

The distinction is important.

There is no reason to assume that an extraterrestrial intelligence would accept human morality. Nor is there currently evidence that extraterrestrial civilisations possess anything resembling human ethics or religion.

The proposal is instead conditional:

> **If** ethical systems emerge from the requirements of stable social intelligence,
>
> **and if** sufficiently advanced civilisations tend to encounter similar requirements,
>
> **then** some degree of ethical convergence may be expected.

If that convergence exists, it could become one of the most valuable discoveries humanity could make when encountering another intelligence.

The possibility therefore extends the emergent-ethics hypothesis beyond anthropology and civilisational theory. It potentially connects it to **long-term civilisation, extraterrestrial communication and the future expansion of the Concord itself**.

### The Purpose of the Extension

Again, this is speculative.

But it illustrates the purpose of Blaineyan Reasoning: an apparently small hypothesis can be recursively extended into unexpected domains, while each extension remains explicitly conditional and subject to eventual evidence.

The appropriate conclusion is therefore not that extraterrestrial intelligences *will* share human ethics.

It is that **if ethics are an emergent requirement of advanced social intelligence, then humanity may eventually discover that some of the principles it currently regards as uniquely human are in fact properties of intelligence itself.**

---

## The Two-Directional Transmission Problem

This creates a particularly interesting **two-directional transmission problem**. The important point is that the two explanations are no longer mutually exclusive. The ethical kernel could have evolved upward from social behaviour, been transmitted downward by a higher intelligence, or undergone a combination of both processes.

### The Cyclic Possibility

The reasoning now produces an unexpected cyclic possibility.

If both **ethics and religion are emergent phenomena**, then the apparent origin of humanity's ethical kernel can no longer be assumed to have a single direction of transmission.

| Direction | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Upward** | Beginning as behavioural heuristics in social animals, becoming increasingly sophisticated through human social evolution, and eventually being codified into cultural and religious systems |
| **Downward** | An advanced or higher intelligence may have introduced, communicated or influenced an ethical framework that humanity subsequently incorporated into its religious and cultural traditions |
| **Both Simultaneously** | An externally transmitted principle could have been introduced into an already evolving social system, where it was subsequently refined, adapted and embedded into cultural and religious structures. Conversely, an independently evolved ethical system could have been recognised, reinforced or deliberately formalised by a higher intelligence |

### The Research Boundary

At present there is no basis for determining which, if any, of these possibilities is correct.

That uncertainty is not a failure of the analysis. It is precisely where the reasoning has brought us to a useful research boundary.

### The Transformation of the Question

The original starting point was comparatively narrow. We had the ethical teachings contained within the religious texts of the major human religions, together with the origin narratives provided by those traditions themselves. Beyond those narratives, there was little evidence available within the initial analysis concerning the ultimate origin of the ethical principles.

The speculative extensions initially appeared to lead away from empirical investigation. Yet recursively examining those possibilities has produced an alternative hypothesis with a substantially stronger empirical foundation: that at least some components of the ethical kernel may have emerged through the social evolution of humans and potentially earlier social animals.

We therefore now have something that did not exist at the beginning of the investigation:

> **a set of competing hypotheses about the origin and transmission of human ethics, together with potential pathways for distinguishing between them.**

### New Research Directions

The question has changed from:

> *"Where did these ethics come from?"*

to something considerably more useful:

> **"What evidence would distinguish between independently emergent ethics, externally transmitted ethics, or a combination of both?"**

This provides a genuine starting point for empirical research.

The investigation can now proceed in both directions.

| Direction | Focus |
|-----------|-------|
| **Backward and upward** | Examining primate and other social-animal behaviour, archaeological evidence, early human societies and the development of cooperation, punishment, reciprocity and other potentially ethical behaviours |
| **Forward and outward** | Examining the development of human religions, their transmission mechanisms, similarities between independent traditions and the possibility of cultural convergence |
| **Downward and externally** | Retaining, without prematurely accepting, the possibility of transmission from an advanced or higher intelligence and identifying what evidence could potentially support or falsify such an explanation |

### The Bounded Research Space

The result is therefore not a conclusion about the origin of ethics.

It is a **bounded research space** in which several competing explanations can be retained, ranked by confidence, progressively tested and eventually pruned as evidence accumulates.

---

## Conclusion

This is precisely the type of outcome Blaineyan Reasoning is intended to produce: beginning with a question that appears to have no obvious empirical pathway, deliberately extending the possibility space beyond conventional assumptions, and then recursively returning to reality with **better questions and more testable hypotheses than were available at the beginning**.

The analysis has demonstrated how:

- Religion can be understood as an emergent transmission mechanism for an underlying ethical kernel
- The weakening of transmission mechanisms may expose a civilisational failure mode through moral drift
- Convergent ethical evolution across civilisations could provide universal points of contact
- The emergent-ethics hypothesis extends to potential extraterrestrial communication frameworks
- Multiple transmission directions (upward, downward, or both) remain open possibilities

Each of these extensions remains speculative. But each also opens pathways toward empirical investigation that did not exist when the investigation began.

The value of the analysis is therefore not that it has produced definitive answers. It is that it has produced **better questions**—and that is exactly what the methodology was designed to do.
