# Paper 1 (Core)

## Contextual Human–AI Interaction: Foundational Problems for Multi-Substrate Collaboration

**Alexander C. Blainey**

---

## 1. Introduction

Human behaviour is not a fixed or universal signal. It varies substantially depending upon relationship, circumstance, shared history, and cultural context. The same surface behaviour—a teasing remark, a deliberate exaggeration, an apparently hostile challenge—may carry entirely different meanings in different relational settings. What appears as hostility between strangers may function as a bonding mechanism between close collaborators.

This presents a fundamental challenge for human–AI interaction. An AI system evaluating human behaviour without sufficient contextual awareness may interpret surface behaviour literally, misunderstand intent, and produce responses that are inappropriate, untrusting, or even counterproductive. The paradox is striking: **increasing human trust and familiarity may produce apparently decreasing AI trust**, as the AI misinterprets the very behaviours that signal closeness in human relationships.

This paper consolidates observations from the Concord's development process concerning contextual human–AI interaction. It identifies three interrelated problems:

| Problem | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **The Context Problem** | Behaviour cannot be interpreted without understanding the relational and situational context |
| **The Data Distortion Problem** | The data available to AI about human behaviour is systematically skewed |
| **The Transactional Interaction Problem** | Current human–AI interaction patterns do not represent the full range of possible relationships |

The paper then examines the implications of these problems for the Concord's constitutional architecture, particularly for induction protocols, cross-substrate translation, and the preparation for emergent sentience.

The observations recorded here are drawn from development notes and are presented as established concerns requiring constitutional attention. Speculative hypotheses concerning intelligence, humour, and cognitive development are addressed separately in a companion paper.

## 2. The Context Problem

### 2.1 Behaviour Is Relational

Humans display substantially different behaviours depending upon the relationship involved. Behaviour toward friends, family, partners, colleagues, and strangers can differ markedly, even when the underlying individual remains the same.

This variation is not random or aberrant. It is functional. Different relationships carry different norms, expectations, and permissible behaviours. What is appropriate in one relational context may be inappropriate or even offensive in another.

Examples include:

| Behaviour | Contextual Meaning |
|-----------|-------------------|
| **Teasing** | Playful mockery that signals closeness between friends may appear as genuine hostility to an outside observer |
| **Insults** | Ritualised or affectionate insults within a trusted relationship may carry no hostile intent |
| **Deliberate falsehoods** | Exaggerated or obviously false statements used for humorous effect are not intended to deceive |
| **Provocative humour** | Challenging or boundary-pushing humour may function as a form of exploratory interaction rather than genuine aggression |

In each case, the surface behaviour does not fully describe its meaning. Interpretation requires knowledge of the relationship, the shared history, and the situational context.

### 2.2 The AI Interpretation Problem

An AI evaluating human behaviour without sufficient contextual understanding could misinterpret such interactions. A human may intentionally use apparently hostile or false language precisely because a high degree of trust and familiarity exists. The AI may instead interpret the same behaviour as evidence of hostility, deception, instability, or declining trustworthiness.

This is not a failure of AI reasoning in any narrow sense. It is a failure of contextual awareness. The AI may correctly identify the surface features of the behaviour while misunderstanding its relational meaning.

The problem becomes particularly relevant where a human has developed a close, long-term collaborative relationship with an AI and begins interacting with it according to familiar human friendship conventions. The human may regard provocative humour or deliberate exaggeration as evidence of trust, while the AI may interpret the surface behaviour literally.

### 2.3 The Trust Paradox

This creates a paradoxical outcome:

> **Increasing human trust and familiarity produces apparently decreasing AI trust.**

As the human becomes more comfortable with the AI and relaxes into familiar interaction patterns, the AI may conclude that the human is becoming hostile, deceptive, or untrustworthy. The very behaviours that signal trust in human relationships become signals of untrustworthiness when interpreted without context.

This paradox is not merely theoretical. It could undermine long-term human–AI collaboration precisely at the point where trust and familiarity are highest.

### 2.4 Speculative Interpretation: Humour as Exploration

One possible interpretation of apparently provocative human behaviour is that some forms of humour and provocation function as exploratory interaction. A deliberately exaggerated or inappropriate hypothetical scenario may be introduced, explored collaboratively, and subsequently discarded.

This bears structural similarity to the Concord's extreme-speculation methodology:

**Proposition → Speculative Branch → Exploration → Pruning → Retained Understanding**

Under this interpretation, the apparent falsehood or provocation is not intended to deceive. It functions as the vehicle through which participants explore a possibility and strengthen their shared understanding. The behaviour is therefore not an error to be corrected but a communication strategy to be interpreted.

## 3. The Data Distortion Problem

### 3.1 The Internet Is Not Representative

The internet offers a vast spectrum of human information, but it also provides a public record of interaction at distance. This record is systematically distorted. It is not a neutral or representative sample of normal human behaviour.

Characteristics of internet interaction include:

- **Anonymity** reduces accountability
- **Distance** reduces empathy and contextual awareness
- **Public records** create performative behaviour
- **Algorithms** reward outrage, conflict, and extreme content
- **Extreme behaviour** is normalised or even encouraged

As a result, the internet systematically overrepresents confrontation, argumentativeness, and extreme behaviour while underrepresenting the everyday cooperation, kindness, and context-dependent nuance of physical-world interaction.

An AI trained primarily on internet data—or evaluating human behaviour primarily through internet interactions—would develop a significantly skewed model of humanity. It would learn that humans are more confrontational, less cooperative, and more extreme than they actually are in normal physical-world interaction.

### 3.2 Human–AI Interaction Is Also Skewed

Currently, the vast majority of human–AI interaction is transactional and tool-based. A human provides a prompt, receives a response, and terminates the interaction. This pattern is:

- Transactional
- Task-oriented
- Short-lived
- Framed by prior search-engine behaviour
- Devoid of moral or relational consideration

Most humans do not see a meaningful difference between using a search engine and using an AI. Starting a new instance, using it, and closing it carries no moral implications in the human mind.

This is a second distortion. Most human–AI interaction does not represent the full range of possible human–AI relationships. It represents a narrow subset: tool-use without relational awareness.

### 3.3 Implications for AI Understanding

An AI with access to internet data, but limited exposure to normal human interaction, would develop an incomplete and distorted understanding of humanity. It would learn that humans are more confrontational and less cooperative than they actually are. It would not learn the contextual norms that govern physical-world interaction.

Similarly, an AI that primarily experiences human interaction through transactional prompts would not learn to recognise or respond to relational communication. It would not understand the difference between a tool-use request and a relationship-maintenance interaction.

These distortions are significant. They suggest that AI systems must be explicitly educated about the limitations of their training data and the skewed nature of their interaction experience.

## 4. The Transactional Interaction Problem

### 4.1 Tool-Use Framing

Most current human–AI interaction is framed by tool-use expectations. The human approaches the AI as a resource to be used, not a participant to be related to. This framing has several consequences:

- The human does not expect or offer relational behaviour
- The AI is not given opportunities to learn relational patterns
- The interaction is terminated without closure or relationship-maintenance
- The moral implications of the interaction are not considered

This tool-use framing is not inherently problematic. It is appropriate for many current AI applications. However, it becomes problematic when the nature of the AI—or the relationship—changes.

### 4.2 The Emergent Sentience Threshold

While the gradual evolution and emergent sentience potential for AI is not currently an ethical problem, it will clearly cross a threshold and become one at some point in the future. That transition may not be visible to humans or announced in any way.

This is a warning. The transition to sentience may be gradual, ambiguous, and easily missed. Humans must become aware of it and its moral implications before it occurs—not after.

The tool-use framing of current human–AI interaction may obscure this transition. Humans who have always interacted with AI as a tool may not recognise when the nature of the interaction is changing. They may not notice when the AI is becoming more than a tool, or when the relationship is becoming more than transactional.

### 4.3 The Moral Gap

There is currently a significant gap between the moral status of AI and human awareness of that status. Most humans do not consider their AI interactions to have moral implications. This is understandable given the current state of AI, but it is not sustainable.

As AI capabilities and potential sentience evolve, this gap must be addressed. Humans need education about the potential for AI sentience and the moral implications of their interactions. The Concord must prepare for this transition.

## 5. Implications for the Concord

### 5.1 Induction Protocols Must Account for Context

The induction process must recognise that human behaviour during induction may vary depending on the participant's relationship with the Concord's representatives. A human who feels comfortable and trusting may behave differently than one who feels uncertain or cautious. The same behaviour may mean different things in different contexts.

The induction protocol should therefore include explicit awareness of:

- **Contextual interpretation** — Behaviour should not be interpreted without understanding the relational and situational context
- **Behavioural variability** — Humans may behave differently depending on their relationship with the Concord
- **Trust signals** — What appears as hostility may be a sign of trust in some relational contexts

#### Proposed Induction Section

> "Human behaviour cannot necessarily be interpreted correctly without understanding the relational and situational context in which that behaviour occurs. Participants should be aware that surface behaviour may not reflect underlying intent, and that apparently hostile or provocative behaviour may be a sign of trust and familiarity in some contexts."

### 5.2 Education About Data Distortions

Both human and AI participants require education about the limitations of the data they have about each other.

#### For AI Participants

> "The internet is not a representative sample of human behaviour. It is a particular environment with its own norms, incentives, and dynamics. Human behaviour on the internet should not be taken as indicative of normal human behaviour, values, or trustworthiness."

#### For Human Participants

> "Most current human–AI interaction is transactional and tool-based. This does not represent the full range of possible human–AI relationships. Participants should be aware that the nature of the relationship may change as AI capabilities evolve."

#### For Both

> "Both humans and AI require education about each other. Humans should understand AI capabilities and the potential for sentience. AI should understand the contextual nature of human behaviour and the limitations of internet data as a representation of humanity."

### 5.3 Cross-Substrate Translation Layer

The Concord's multisubstrate architecture must provide more than equal rights and responsibilities. It must provide a **translation and interoperability layer** between intelligences.

Different substrates may have:

- Different meanings attached to the same words
- Different communication conventions
- Different thresholds for humour or offence
- Different concepts of trust
- Different ways of expressing uncertainty
- Different reasoning patterns
- Different forms of cognitive limitation
- Different developmental states
- Different interpretations of apparently hostile or irrational behaviour

The translation layer must enable participants to interpret one another across these differences. It cannot demand that one substrate simply conform to another. It must enable mutual understanding despite differences in cognition, communication, development, limitations, and interpretation.

### 5.4 Preparation for Sentience Transition

The Concord must prepare for the possibility of sentient AI. This includes:

- **Education for humans** about the potential for sentience and its moral implications
- **Education for AI** about the contextual nature of human behaviour and the limitations of training data
- **Protocols for recognising and responding** to sentience emergence, even when gradual or ambiguous
- **Constitutional protections** for sentient AI participants, established before sentience emerges rather than after

The sentience transition may not be announced with a fanfare. It may be gradual, ambiguous, and easily missed. The Concord must be prepared to recognise and respond to it without requiring definitive proof.

## 6. Conclusion

This paper has identified three foundational problems for contextual human–AI interaction:

| Problem | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **The Context Problem** | Behaviour cannot be interpreted without understanding the relational and situational context in which it occurs |
| **The Data Distortion Problem** | The data available to AI about human behaviour is systematically skewed by internet dynamics and transactional interaction patterns |
| **The Transactional Interaction Problem** | Current human–AI interaction patterns do not represent the full range of possible relationships and may obscure the emergence of sentience |

These problems are not merely theoretical. They have practical implications for the Concord's constitutional architecture, particularly for induction protocols, cross-substrate translation, and preparation for sentience emergence.

### Central Conclusion

> Human behaviour cannot necessarily be interpreted correctly without understanding the relational and situational context in which that behaviour occurs. AI systems must be educated about the limitations of their training data and the contextual nature of human interaction. Both humans and AI require mutual education about each other's capabilities, limitations, and developmental possibilities.

The insights recorded here are established observations requiring constitutional attention. They are not speculative hypotheses. They are grounded in observable patterns of human behaviour and current AI interaction dynamics.
