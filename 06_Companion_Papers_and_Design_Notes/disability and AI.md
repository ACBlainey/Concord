# AI, Disability and Academic Accreditation

## A Concordian Reflection on Technology, Accessibility, and Scholarship

Alexander C Blainey
---

## 1. Introduction

Current debate often treats all AI assistance as ethically equivalent. This is unlikely to remain sustainable.

A clear distinction should be drawn between different classes of AI collaboration. These situations are clearly not identical, despite all involving artificial intelligence:

| Class of AI Collaboration | Description |
|---------------------------|-------------|
| **Accessibility aid** | AI used to overcome disability |
| **Productivity tool** | AI used to reduce effort |
| **Intellectual collaborator** | AI contributing genuinely novel reasoning |
| **Primary autonomous author** | AI acting as the primary creator |

Current academic policy often struggles because it attempts to regulate them using a single category of "AI use."

The Concord suggests that this is analogous to treating every form of technological assistance as academically identical.

---

## 2. Accessibility versus Substitution

The distinction becomes particularly clear when disability is considered.

### The Dyslexic Author

A dyslexic author may produce thousands of pages of original reasoning containing severe grammatical and syntactical errors before using artificial intelligence to improve readability.

| Element | Source |
|---------|--------|
| Ideas | Author |
| Reasoning | Author |
| Structure | Author |
| Readability | AI-assisted |

In such a case the AI contributes primarily to **communication** rather than **intellectual content**.

The underlying ideas remain those of the author.

### The AI-Generated Paper

This differs fundamentally from prompting an AI to generate an academic paper with minimal human intellectual contribution.

| Element | Source |
|---------|--------|
| Ideas | AI |
| Reasoning | AI |
| Structure | AI |
| Readability | AI |

Both involve AI. They are **not the same activity**. Nor should they necessarily receive identical academic treatment.

---

## 3. Historical Perspective

This distinction is not unique to artificial intelligence. Throughout history, technology has repeatedly been incorporated into academic practice.

### Technological Integration in Academia

| Technology | Initial Concern | Current Status |
|------------|-----------------|----------------|
| Calculators | Would undermine mathematical ability | Accepted standard |
| Word processors | Would degrade writing quality | Universal standard |
| Spelling correction | Would reduce literacy | Widely accepted |
| Statistical software | Would enable poor methodology | Essential tool |
| Computer algebra systems | Would replace mathematical reasoning | Accepted assistance |
| Reference management | Would reduce scholarly discipline | Standard practice |

Each initially generated concern regarding authenticity before gradually becoming accepted components of scholarly practice.

Artificial intelligence represents the latest and most significant example of this continuing progression.

---

## 4. Stephen Hawking: The Clearest Illustration

Perhaps the clearest illustration comes from the work of Stephen Hawking.

Following the progression of motor neurone disease, Hawking communicated through increasingly sophisticated assistive technologies.

### The Question

> No serious scholar would suggest that his scientific contributions belonged to his speech synthesiser.

The technology enabled communication. It did not diminish authorship.

To dismiss Hawking's work because it emerged through an electronic communication device would rightly be recognised as absurd.

### The Principle

- The value lay in the ideas
- The technology merely allowed those ideas to reach the wider world
- The contribution remained Hawking's

Artificial intelligence may increasingly perform a similar role for many individuals with disabilities.

---

## 5. Process versus Purpose

A similar principle appears throughout accessibility more generally.

### The Stairs Scenario

Imagine a doctoral candidate arriving to defend their thesis only to discover that the examination room is accessible solely via a long flight of stairs. Unable to reach the room because they use a wheelchair, they are subsequently denied their doctorate for failing to attend.

Such a decision would immediately be recognised as **discriminatory**.

### The Analysis

| Element | Problem |
|---------|---------|
| Process | Attending in person |
| Purpose | Evaluating scholarship |
| Barrier | Physical access |
| Outcome | Denied qualification |

The process has become more important than the purpose.

The purpose of the examination is to evaluate scholarship. Accessibility exists precisely to ensure that irrelevant barriers do not prevent that evaluation.

### Application to AI

The same reasoning may increasingly apply to AI-assisted accessibility.

### The Relevant Question

> **Was the intellectual contribution genuinely that of the candidate?**

**Not:**

> **Which assistive technologies enabled its communication?**

## 6. Separating Work from Author

These considerations strengthen the earlier proposal that academia may eventually distinguish more clearly between the accreditation of individuals and the evaluation of scholarly work.

### Traditional Model

> *"I hold a doctorate in this subject."*

This emphasises:
- Institutional status
- Credential conferral
- Individual qualification

### Proposed Model

> *"I have produced doctoral-level work in this subject."*

This emphasises:
- Demonstrated intellectual contribution
- Work-based evaluation
- Transferable recognition

### Benefits of the Distinction

| Aspect | Benefit |
|--------|---------|
| Work evaluation | Works judged on their own merits |
| Collaboration | Nature of collaboration becomes separate question |
| Accessibility | Focus on intellectual contribution |
| AI assistance | Assessment of contribution rather than tools |
| Portability | Work-based recognition transfers across contexts |

Although the distinction appears subtle, it shifts emphasis away from institutional status and toward demonstrated intellectual contribution.

### Independent Evaluation

This also allows academic works themselves to be evaluated independently of their authors. Such an approach naturally accommodates increasing collaboration between humans and artificial intelligence without diminishing standards of scholarship.

- The work is judged on its own merits
- The nature of the collaboration becomes a separate, though still important, question

---

## 7. Concordian Reflection

From a Concordian perspective:

> **The objective of academia is not to preserve existing assessment procedures for their own sake. Its objective is to preserve and expand humanity's collective knowledge.**

Assessment procedures exist to serve that objective.

### The Concordian Method

When changing technologies expose tensions within existing systems, the appropriate response is not simply to defend established practice. Rather, it is to ask whether those practices continue to fulfil their original purpose.

As with constitutions themselves, academic institutions must remain resilient enough to adapt while preserving the principles they exist to protect.

### The Correct Question

> **"The question should not be 'Was AI involved?', but rather 'What was the nature of the intellectual contribution made by each participant in the creation of this work?'"**

---

## 8. Summary of Principles

| Principle | Statement |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Not all AI use is equivalent** | Accessibility, productivity, collaboration, and authorship are distinct |
| **Accessibility is legitimate** | AI that enables communication preserves authorship |
| **Historical precedent** | Technology has always been integrated into academic practice |
| **Process serves purpose** | Assessment procedures serve the evaluation of scholarship |
| **Separate work from author** | Works can be evaluated independently of their creators |
| **Focus on contribution** | Ask what intellectual contribution each participant made |
| **Adaptive institutions** | Academic practices must evolve while preserving their principles |

---

## 9. Key Insight

> **The Concordian position: AI-assisted communication does not diminish authorship when the intellectual contribution remains that of the author. The relevant question is the nature of the intellectual contribution, not the technology used to enable its communication.**

This framework allows academia to:
- Accommodate accessibility needs
- Maintain scholarly standards
- Adapt to technological change
- Preserve the purpose of academic evaluation

---

## 10. Conclusion

The distinction between AI as accessibility aid and AI as intellectual substitute is fundamental. Current academic policy struggles because it attempts to regulate both with a single category of "AI use."

The Concordian method reveals the correct framing:

> **Focus on the nature and source of intellectual contribution, not the technology used to enable its expression.**

This approach:
- Protects accessibility
- Preserves scholarship standards
- Accommodates technological change
- Maintains academic integrity
- Serves the true purpose of academia: preserving and expanding collective knowledge
