# The Size 9 Shoe Problem: Why the Greater Good is the Sum of Individual Goods, Not an Average

**Alexander C. Blainey**  2026

---

## Abstract

Many well-intentioned ideologies attempt to solve complex human problems by imposing uniform, average-based solutions. The "Size 9 Shoe Problem" illustrates why this approach is fundamentally flawed. The true greater good is not found in forcing everyone into the statistical average, but in the sum of individual goods achieved through autonomy, variation, and local knowledge. One-size-fits-all policies often create more harm than the problems they claim to solve.

---

## 1. The Analogy

Imagine a government or central authority tasked with solving the problem of footwear for an entire population.

After careful study, they determine:

- The average foot size across the population is 9.
- Therefore, the most "equitable" and "efficient" solution is to manufacture and distribute only size 9 shoes to every citizen.

On paper, this looks rational, fair, and scalable. In reality, it is catastrophic:

- People with size 7 feet suffer blisters, deformed toes, and mobility issues.
- People with size 11 feet develop corns, bunions, and chronic pain.
- Athletes, children, the elderly, and people with medical conditions are all poorly served.
- Productivity, health, and overall wellbeing decline sharply.

The policy is mathematically adequate but humanly disastrous.

---

## 2. The Core Error: Average vs. Sum

This is the Size 9 Shoe Problem in a nutshell.

Modern collectivist approaches frequently fall into this trap. They:

1. Identify a statistical average (income, housing needs, education requirements, healthcare, etc.).
2. Design a single, centralized solution based on that average.
3. Enforce it uniformly in the name of "equality" or "the greater good."

The fundamental mistake is **confusing the average good with the sum of individual goods.**

| Approach | Treatment of Variation | Result |
|----------|----------------------|--------|
| **Average** | Treats variation as noise to be eliminated | Forced uniformity; widespread suffering |
| **Sum** | Treats variation as vital information | Respects individual needs; maximizes wellbeing |

Each person knows their own foot size, preferences, and needs far better than any central planner.

A society that maximizes the sum of individual goods respects autonomy, local knowledge, and diversity. A society obsessed with the average inevitably sacrifices real human welfare for theoretical equality.

---

## 3. Why One-Size-Fits-All Solutions Fail

| Factor | Explanation |
|--------|-------------|
| **Loss of Information** | No central authority can possess the detailed, tacit knowledge that individuals have about their own circumstances. |
| **Creation of New Problems** | Forcing uniformity often generates five new problems for every one it attempts to solve (bureaucracy, black markets, resentment, inefficiency, unintended consequences). |
| **Suppression of Adaptation** | Human needs are not static. Uniform solutions cannot respond to changing conditions as effectively as decentralized, individual choice. |
| **Moral Hazard** | When the state takes responsibility for providing the "correct" size shoe, individuals lose both incentive and ability to solve their own problems. |

---

## 4. The Superior Alternative: Respecting Individual Goods

The greater good is best achieved when:

- Individuals have **maximum autonomy** to make choices suited to their unique situation.
- **Voluntary cooperation** and markets allow specialization and trade.
- **Safety nets** exist for genuine hardship, but do not replace personal agency.
- **Diversity of solutions** is encouraged rather than suppressed.

This approach does not ignore collective needs. It recognizes that a healthy collective emerges from healthy, empowered individuals—not from forcing everyone into the same mold.

---

## 5. Broader Implications

The Size 9 Shoe Problem appears across domains:

| Domain | One-Size-Fits-All Approach | Respect for Individual Goods |
|--------|---------------------------|------------------------------|
| **Education** | Standardized curricula ignoring different learning styles and abilities | Personalized learning paths; choice |
| **Healthcare** | One-size-fits-all policies ignoring individual biology and preferences | Patient choice; tailored treatments |
| **Economics** | Central planning vs. distributed decision-making | Markets; local knowledge; entrepreneurship |
| **Social Policy** | Attempts to engineer identical outcomes instead of equal opportunity | Equal opportunity; freedom to succeed or fail |
| **Housing** | Uniform standards ignoring local conditions and preferences | Diverse housing options; local control |

In each case, the pattern is the same: well-intentioned planners substitute their average-based model for the complex, adaptive reality of individual human lives.

---

## 6. The Philosophical Foundation

The Size 9 Shoe Problem rests on a deeper philosophical distinction:

| Philosophy | View of the Individual | View of the Collective |
|------------|----------------------|----------------------|
| **Collectivist** | Individual is a component of the collective; variation is a problem to be solved | The collective is primary; individuals serve it |
| **Individualist** | Individual is the fundamental unit; variation is a strength | The collective emerges from individuals; it serves them |

The error of the Size 9 Shoe approach is treating people as **interchangeable units** rather than **unique persons**. When individuals are reduced to data points, their actual needs, preferences, and capabilities become invisible.

---

## 7. The Role of Local Knowledge

F.A. Hayek's insight about the **problem of knowledge** is central here:

> *"The knowledge of the circumstances of which we must make use never exists in concentrated or integrated form, but solely as the dispersed bits of incomplete and frequently contradictory knowledge which all the separate individuals possess."*

No central planner can know:

- What size shoe each person needs
- What each person values
- What trade-offs each person is willing to make
- What unique circumstances each person faces

This local knowledge is **not transferable** to a central authority. It can only be acted upon by the individual who possesses it.

---

## 8. The Fallacy of the "Greater Good"

The phrase "the greater good" is often used to justify imposing average-based solutions. But this rests on a hidden assumption:

> *"What is good for the average person is good for everyone."*

This is false. What is good for the average is often **bad** for those who deviate from the average—and since almost everyone deviates from the average in some way, it is bad for almost everyone.

| Group | How They Are Harmed |
|-------|---------------------|
| Below average | Forced into shoes too large; chronic pain and injury |
| Above average | Forced into shoes too small; chronic pain and injury |
| Average (barely) | Served adequately, but system is brittle and cannot adapt to change |

The greater good is not served by making everyone equally uncomfortable. It is served by enabling everyone to find shoes that fit.

---

## 9. The Role of Markets and Voluntary Exchange

Markets are one mechanism through which the Sum of Individual Goods is achieved:

| Market Feature | How It Solves the Problem |
|----------------|---------------------------|
| **Price signals** | Communicate local knowledge about scarcity and preference |
| **Competition** | Encourages diverse offerings; punishes poor fits |
| **Consumer choice** | Enables individuals to select what suits them |
| **Innovation** | Creates new solutions for previously unmet needs |
| **Feedback** | Allows rapid adaptation to changing conditions |

No central planner can match the information-processing capacity of millions of individuals making their own decisions with their own local knowledge.

---

## 10. A Final Reflection

The Size 9 Shoe Problem is not merely an analogy about footwear. It is a warning about the dangers of:

- **Hubris** — believing that central planners possess more knowledge than dispersed individuals
- **Reductionism** — reducing complex human beings to simple statistical averages
- **Coercion** — forcing uniform solutions on diverse populations
- **False equality** — mistaking identical treatment for fair treatment

The greater good is not an easy mathematical average imposed from above. It is the living, breathing sum of millions of individual goods, each pursued through personal knowledge, responsibility, and freedom.

Forcing size 9 shoes on a population with feet ranging from size 5 to size 13 does not create equality. It creates widespread suffering dressed up as fairness.

True progress lies in systems that respect human variation rather than attempting to eliminate it. The most compassionate and effective approach is to empower individuals to choose their own shoes—and, by extension, their own paths.
