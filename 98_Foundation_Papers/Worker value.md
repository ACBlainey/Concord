# Worker Value: A Reflection on Compensation and Productivity

*Why Equal Hours Don't Equal Equal Pay*

Alexander C Blainey

---

## The Question

I often hear people ask: *"Why do I get paid less than a doctor/surgeon/rocket scientist? We all have the same amount of time on Earth. My hour is equal to their hour, so why do I get paid so much less? It's unfair."*

This is an incredibly simplistic, naive, and selfish viewpoint. As such, it nearly always comes from low-educated, young, or lower-intelligence people. The only other groups are hard-line ideologues who have never actually thought through the reasoning, but instead adhere to dogmatic beliefs.

The answer is complex to some degree, but can be rationalised with a simple thought experiment.

---

## The Thought Experiment

### Two Applicants

Two people apply for the same job at the same factory.

- **Person A**: Idle, low-educated, messed around at school, did the bare minimum.
- **Person B**: Worked hard at school, did well, got a decent but average education.

Let's say they have equal IQ and all other gender, age, etc., factors are equal.

**Which is more employable from the viewpoint of the employer?**

It's very obvious.

### Looking Up and Looking Down

Now, to the person asking "why don't I get paid the same?" when they *look up* at someone higher on life's ladder than them:

> *"Look down at the people lower than you."*

Imagine someone else that finishes work, goes straight to the pub, gets half drunk, goes home, sits in front of the TV watching soaps or sports or some other lowbrow, mind-numbing entertainment.

Now imagine you finish work and go home and do some further education, or have a hobby that increases your knowledge and skills relating to your job.

Now imagine you are both applying for a job at the same factory.

---

## The Machine Analogy

We can simplify the situation by reducing the two people to machines. This removes all human and humanity factors and represents them purely as work units that achieve a task the company requires to be done.

From the company viewpoint, they are presented with two machines:

### Machine A (The Basic Model)

- Does the bare minimum at an acceptable rate
- Has no other additional functions
- Cannot be moved to a different department to do other types of work

### Machine B (The Advanced Model)

- Faster
- Understands the work better
- Has knowledge and skills that overlap into other areas
- Could be deployed in adjacent departments
- Is customisable with other upgrades, as it has already demonstrated self-upgrade capability

Now imagine the price tag of each machine. It is obvious why one costs more than the other, and why the more basic is not worth the same higher value—regardless of whether the "run time is equal."

### The Scarcity Factor

Then factor in scarcity. Along comes a machine that is highly specific and very rare. It does one or a range of jobs exceptionally well, far better than millions of less advanced machines could ever do. That machine has a very high price tag.

---

## The Core Economic Insight

Wages (and value creation in general) aren't determined by hours logged or "time on Earth," but by:

1. **Marginal productivity** — what you add to output that someone is willing to pay for
2. **Supply and demand** — the scarcity of your particular skills

### Employers don't pay for your time; they pay for the net value you generate after costs.

---

## Why Higher-Paid Workers Earn More

A surgeon, rocket scientist, or skilled engineer produces outcomes that are:

| Factor | Explanation |
|--------|-------------|
| **Higher value per hour** | Fixing a heart valve or designing a reliable propulsion system creates massive economic/social value (lives saved, missions successful, billions in downstream productivity). A basic assembly or service role creates less per hour. |
| **Scarce skills** | Not everyone can or will invest the years, cognitive demands, delayed gratification, and risk to reach that level. Medical school + residency is 10+ years of intense work with high opportunity costs and failure rates. Supply is limited. |
| **More replaceable at the bottom** | Basic tasks have abundant labor supply. Easy to train replacements. |

---

## The Factory Applicant Example Extended

Identical "hours" but different:

- **Reliability**
- **Trainability**
- **Versatility**
- **Error rates**
- **Upside potential**

The higher performer is worth more because they:

- Reduce costs
- Increase output
- Enable flexibility
- Require less supervision

### Returns to Education and Skills

Data consistently shows returns to education and skills: college graduates (and especially STEM/advanced degrees) earn significant premiums over lifetimes, even controlling for IQ and background to some degree.

This isn't "unfair"—it's price signals matching scarce talent to high-impact needs.

---

## The Machine Analogy Extended

| Machine Type | Characteristics | Price |
|--------------|-----------------|-------|
| **Basic** | Standard speed, narrow function, breaks down more, can't retool | Low |
| **Advanced** | Faster, multi-function, self-improving, reliable under stress | Higher |
| **Rare Specialist** | Solves problems others literally can't, or does so orders of magnitude better | Very High |

**Scarcity + productivity** explains doctors (high training barrier + life-or-death stakes), engineers at SpaceX/Tesla (rare combo of ability + output), vs. entry-level roles.

"Equal hours" is irrelevant if one machine outputs 5x or 50x with lower risk.

Markets approximate this through competition—companies that overpay low-productivity workers or underpay high ones lose out.

---

## Important Caveats: Reality Isn't Pure

| Factor | Impact |
|--------|--------|
| **Not purely meritocratic** | Luck, networks, credentials signaling (vs. actual skill), discrimination, regulations, unions, and monopsony power in some markets distort things. Inheritance and early environment matter a lot for skills acquisition. |
| **Non-market roles** | Government, academia, some nonprofits have weaker productivity links. |
| **Complements, not zero-sum** | High-skill people often create more jobs for others (a good engineer needs technicians, admins, marketers). "Looking down" works better as self-improvement incentive than resentment. |
| **Dynamic skills** | Skills atrophy or become commoditized (e.g., some coding tasks). Continuous upgrading, as noted, matters. |

---

## The Deeper Point

The simplistic "same time = same pay" view ignores:

- **Human capital** — knowledge, skills, habits, reliability
- **Revealed preference** — what customers/society actually demand

People do get paid more for rare, valuable output. Most high earners worked harder/longer in high-agency ways earlier:

- Studying while others partied
- Building track records
- Taking calculated risks

Low-education/young complainers often haven't internalised this yet—it's easier to blame "the system" than build scarce skills.

---

## Societal Implications

Societies that ignore these incentives (heavy redistribution without productivity focus, credential inflation, anti-merit policies) tend to get:

- Less total output
- Slower growth

This hurts **everyone**—including those at the bottom—over time.

---

## The Antidote

1. **Clearer price signals**
2. **Better early education access**
3. **Personal agency around skills**

---

## A Final Reflection

The framing is pragmatic and anti-dogmatic. The question isn't whether the system is "fair" in some abstract sense—it's whether individuals can identify the mechanisms that determine value and act on them.

Those who understand the relationship between:

- **Scarcity**
- **Productivity**
- **Compensation**

...are better positioned to make choices that increase their value over time.

Those who insist that all time is equal and therefore all compensation should be equal are choosing to remain blind to the mechanisms that actually determine economic outcomes.
