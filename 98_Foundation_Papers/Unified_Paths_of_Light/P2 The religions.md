# Sacred Codes and Civilizational Longevity

## A Comparative Framework for Extracting Universal Ethics from the World's Enduring Religions

**Author:** Alexander C. Blainey  
**Date:** 2026  
**Paper:** II of IV

---

## 1. Introduction

Having established in Paper I the hypothesis that enduring religions function as transmission vehicles for prosocial ethical heuristics that may correlate with civilizational stability, we now turn to the operational question:

> **Which religions will be included in this comparative analysis?**

The selection of cases is a methodological decision of primary importance, as it determines the scope of subsequent ethical extraction and cross-comparison.

This paper defines the selection criteria, justifies the exclusion of certain traditions, and presents the final list of eight religions. The goal is not to produce an exhaustive catalogue of all faiths, but to identify a representative set that satisfies two concurrent conditions:

1. Large global adherent base
2. Demonstrable historical endurance

These criteria are chosen to test the hypothesis that **scale and longevity** are the observable outcomes we seek to explain through ethical content.

---

## 2. Selection Criteria

Two formal criteria govern inclusion in this study:

### Criterion A: Global Adherent Population

The religion must rank among the largest by number of contemporary self-identifying adherents. Population size ensures that the religion has demonstrably influenced large numbers of human beings across diverse geographies, providing sufficient "data" for testing the hypothesis that its ethics correlate with stable civilizational outcomes.

> **Threshold:** Approximately **30 million adherents** as a practical lower bound.

This excludes micro-traditions that, while historically important, lack the demographic scale to support generalizable claims about civilizational effects.

### Criterion B: Historical Endurance

The religion must have a continuous, verifiable historical practice of at least **1,500 years**.

This criterion ensures that the religion has survived across multiple political regimes, economic systems, and cultural shifts, thereby offering evidence that its ethical framework has contributed to—or at least not impeded—long-term social cohesion.

> **Traditions founded within the last millennium are excluded** to avoid conflating recent growth with proven durability.

## 4. Discussion of Inclusions

### Christianity
- **Population:** ~2.4–2.6 billion
- **Age:** ~2,000 years
- **Justification:** Largest global religion with profound influence on Western legal and ethical systems
- **Textual Basis:** Bible contains extensive prescriptive material

### Islam
- **Population:** ~1.9–2.1 billion
- **Age:** ~1,400 years
- **Justification:** Second-largest religion with unbroken tradition
- **Textual Basis:** Qur'an and Hadith provide a dense ethical corpus

### Hinduism
- **Population:** ~1.1–1.2 billion
- **Age:** ~3,000+ years (oldest living)
- **Justification:** Extraordinary antiquity; oldest living religion with complete scriptural texts
- **Critical Value:** Offers the longest continuous ethical record among living traditions

### Buddhism
- **Population:** ~500–535 million
- **Age:** ~2,500 years (6th century BCE)
- **Justification:** Significant influence across Asia
- **Distinctive Feature:** Non-theistic ethical framework based on Noble Eightfold Path and precepts

### Chinese Folk Religions (Confucian-Daoist Tradition)
- **Population:** ~450–460 million
- **Age:** ~2,500+ years
- **Justification:** Complex categorisation encompassing Confucian ethics, Daoist practice, and local spirit worship
- **Approach:** Transmitted ethical traditions of Confucianism and Daoism provide textual basis
- **Significance:** Ensures representation of East Asian civilisational ethics

### Sikhism
- **Population:** ~30–31 million
- **Age:** ~550 years (15th century CE)
- **Justification:** Meets population threshold
- **Distinctive Feature:** Younger comparative case demonstrating scale and durability achieved in shorter timeframe

### Judaism
- **Population:** ~15–16 million
- **Age:** ~3,500+ years (oral tradition)
- **Justification:** Exceptional historical endurance despite small population
- **Significance:** Matrix from which Christianity and Islam emerged; tests whether ethical content correlates with resilience rather than mere population size

### African Traditional / Ethnoreligions
- **Population:** ~288–292 million
- **Age:** Variable, often pre-colonial, >1,500 years
- **Justification:** Represents indigenous ethical systems across Africa
- **Approach:** Focus on widely shared principles (ancestor veneration, community reciprocity) documented in anthropological sources

---

## 5. Discussion of Exclusions

### Agnosticism / Atheism / Non-religious
- **Reason:** Not religions providing a positive ethical code transmitted through sacred texts; they are absence-of-belief categories

### New Religious Movements
- **Reason:** Fail the endurance criterion; most post-date 1800

### Bahāʾī (~9 million)
- **Reason:** Falls below population threshold; post-dates endurance criterion (19th century)

### Jainism (~200,000)
- **Reason:** Excluded on population grounds despite notable antiquity

### Zoroastrianism
- **Reason:** Small modern population makes it difficult to test generalisable claims about civilisational outcomes

### Shintō (~2.8 million)
- **Reason:** Excluded on population grounds

## 7. Conclusion and Next Steps

The eight religions selected provide a representative cross-section of humanity's largest and most enduring ethical traditions:

- Span the globe
- Cover multiple millennia
- Include both theistic and non-theistic frameworks

This diversity is essential for testing whether **specific** ethical precepts (e.g., prohibitions on theft or homicide) are universally present, or whether different ethical packages correlate with different forms of civilisational success.

### Next Paper (Paper III)

Will detail the systematic extraction protocol:

1. Identifying each religion's primary sacred text(s)
2. Defining the unit of ethical analysis
3. Applying a standardised reduction procedure to extract plain-English behavioural precepts for subsequent cross-comparison
