# Sacred Codes and Civilizational Longevity

## A Comparative Framework for Extracting Universal Ethics from the World's Enduring Religions

**Author:** Alexander C. Blainey  
**Date:** 2026  
**Paper:** III of V (Abridged Edition)

---

## Introduction to the Extraction Protocol

This paper inaugurates the systematic extraction phase of our project. As outlined in Papers I and II, the goal is to identify, isolate, and compare the core ethical principles embedded in the sacred texts of the world's eight most enduring religions, stripped of theological narrative, divine attribution, and ritual context.

### Methodological Note

> **Exact verbal equivalence is neither expected nor required.**

Ethical principles manifest differently across cultural and linguistic traditions. One text may frame a precept as a prohibition ("Do not steal"), another as a positive injunction ("Give generously"), and yet another as a wisdom saying ("The generous man prospers").

These are not contradictions but **variations in ethical expression**—different cultural lenses focusing on the same underlying behavioural truth. Our task is to identify the **functional ethical core**: what action does the precept prescribe or prohibit, and what social outcome does it promote?

> **This principle of ethical equivalence through diverse expression governs all subsequent comparisons.**

We are building a map of moral commonalities, not a dictionary of identical phrases.

## Psalm 1: The Ethics of Association

### Full Biblical Text

> **Blessed is the man who does not walk in the counsel of the wicked,**
> **Nor stand in the way of sinners,**
> **nor sit in company with scoffers.**
>
> Rather, the law of the LORD is his joy;
> and on his law he meditates day and night.
>
> He is like a tree planted near streams of water,
> that yields its fruit in season;
> Its leaves never wither;
> whatever he does prospers.
>
> But not so are the wicked, not so!
> They are like chaff driven by the wind.
>
> Therefore the wicked will not arise at the judgment,
> nor will sinners in the assembly of the just.
>
> Because the LORD knows the way of the just,
> but the way of the wicked leads to ruin.

---

## Extraction of Core Ethical Lessons

The psalm presents two contrasting "ways" of living—the way of the righteous and the way of the wicked. The ethical content can be reduced to three distinct principles:

| Principle | Ethical Core (Plain English) |
|-----------|------------------------------|
| **Principle 1: Deliberate Avoidance of Negative Influence** | Do not voluntarily place yourself in the company, counsel, or habitual environment of those who act unjustly or mock what is good. Association with wrongdoers leads to the gradual adoption of their ways. |
| **Principle 2: Active Engagement with Righteous Teaching** | Find genuine satisfaction in studying, internalising, and continually reflecting upon moral instruction. This is the source of stability and flourishing. |
| **Principle 3: Consequences Follow Moral Conduct** | The morally upright person, through consistent right action, experiences a stable and flourishing life; the morally corrupt person, through consistent wrong action, experiences instability and eventual ruin. Conduct determines outcome. |

### Focus of Comparison

For our purposes, **Principle 1** and **Principle 2** represent the most actionable ethical heuristics—they prescribe specific behaviours. Principle 3 is a statement of consequentialist ethics (actions have predictable outcomes), which is widely shared but less prescriptive.

We will therefore focus on comparing **Principle 1 (Avoidance of Negative Influence)** across the other seven religions.

## Comparative Analysis: Ethical Equivalents to Psalm 1, Principle 1

| Religion / Text | Ethical Equivalent | Reduced Ethical Core |
|-----------------|-------------------|---------------------|
| **Judaism** | Pirkei Avot 1:7: "Nittai the Arbelite says: Keep far from an evil neighbor, and do not become attached to a wicked person." | Deliberately distance yourself from those who are morally corrupt. Do not form close bonds with them. |
| **Buddhism** | Dhammapada, Yamakavagga: "He, who disregards temperance and truth and who puts on yellow robe without having cleansed himself from sin is unworthy of the yellow robe." Mahābhārata parallel: "An impure person with a yellow robe serves only the interests of cupidity... What counts is inner worth, nobility of soul." | Outward religious identity without inner purity is worthless. True worth is internal, not external. (Corollary: Avoid the company of those who are impure in heart, even if they appear religious.) |
| **Hinduism** | Mahābhārata, Anusāsana Parva: "One should not behave towards others in a disagreeable way. This is the essence of morality. All other activities are due to selfish desire." | Do not act toward others in ways that you would find disagreeable. This is the fundamental principle of moral conduct. (Corollary: Avoid those who act selfishly and without this basic regard for others.) |
| **Islam** | Qur'an, Surah Al-Baqarah (2:177): "Righteousness is not determined by facing East or West during prayer. Righteousness consists of the belief in God... to give money for the love of God to relatives, orphans, the destitute... to set free slaves and to be steadfast in prayer, to pay the religious tax (zakat) to fulfill one's promises, and to exercise patience in poverty, in distress, and in times of war." | True righteousness is demonstrated through concrete actions: charity, prayer, keeping promises, and patience in hardship. The appearance of religiosity (facing East or West) is not what matters. (Corollary: Righteous conduct is defined by action, not affiliation.) |
| **Sikhism** | Guru Granth Sahib (summarised): A moral person is one who has overcome the "five enemies"—lust, anger, greed, attachment, and egotistical pride—through the cultivation of wisdom, truthfulness, justice, temperance, humility, and contentment. | Moral character is defined by mastery over internal vices (lust, greed, pride) and cultivation of virtues (truthfulness, humility). (Corollary: True righteousness is a matter of internal character, not external display. Avoid those who embody the vices.) |
| **Chinese Folk / Confucian** | Confucian Analects (summarised): "The noble person is formed through study, reverence, restraint, sincerity, loyalty, and concern for others." "The underlying logic of [the] understanding [of virtue] serves as a source of ethical inspiration and upliftment." | The exemplary person cultivates virtue through study, sincerity, and concern for others. (Corollary: Seek the company of the virtuous; avoid those who lack these qualities.) |
| **African Traditional** | Setswana proverb: "An individual is a member of the community, and that as an individual he or she achieves higher goals of life not in isolation but through the contribution of the community." | Personal development and moral achievement are inseparable from the community. One becomes what one's community shapes. (Corollary: Therefore, choose a community of good moral character; avoid communities that lack it.) |

## Discussion of Ethical Equivalence

The table above reveals a striking convergence. Across all eight traditions, the same ethical intuition is expressed: **moral character is both influenced by and reflected in one's associations.** The variations in expression are culturally specific, but the behavioural prescription is functionally identical.

| Tradition | Framing of the Principle |
|-----------|--------------------------|
| **Psalm 1** | Progressive warning: do not *walk* with, *stand* with, or *sit* with the wicked—a movement from casual contact to settled habitation. Association is a *process* that shapes character. |
| **Judaism** | Direct imperative: "Keep far from an evil neighbor." No process metaphor; just a command. |
| **Buddhism & Hinduism** | Inner purity vs. outer display. External religious identity is meaningless without internal moral substance. Corollary: avoid those who are impure within. |
| **Islam** | Righteousness is defined by *action*—charity, prayer, patience, keeping promises—not by ritual orientation. Implication: associate with those who *do* these things. |
| **Sikhism** | Internal vices (the "five enemies") and virtues. The moral person conquers greed, anger, and pride. One's companions are those on the same path. |
| **Confucianism** | The "noble person" (*junzi*) cultivates virtue through study and sincerity. Become such a person, and seek such persons. |
| **African Traditional** | The community dimension is explicit: the individual achieves moral goals through the community. Choose your community carefully, for it shapes you. |

---

## Conclusion

Psalm 1, Principle 1—"Avoid the company of the corrupt"—finds clear ethical equivalents in all seven other religions examined. The principle is expressed through different cultural lenses:

- As a progressive warning (Psalm)
- As a direct command (Judaism)
- As a critique of external religiosity (Buddhism/Hinduism)
- As a definition of righteous action (Islam)
- As a list of vices to overcome (Sikhism)
- As a model of the noble person (Confucianism)
- As an affirmation of community responsibility (African Traditional)

> **This convergence supports our central hypothesis: the world's most enduring religions contain a shared ethical core concerning the importance of association and its impact on moral character.**

The ethical lesson transcends its religious packaging.
