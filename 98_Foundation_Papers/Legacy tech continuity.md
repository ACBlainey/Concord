# Continuity of Technology: The Legacy Ladder

*A Reflection on Technological Fragility and the Need for Active Preservation*
Alexander C Blainey
---

## The Problem

We live in a world where we create ever more advanced machines using iterative evolution. Manufacturing machines in a factory produces slightly more advanced and accurate machines that replace themselves. Computer design computer circuits for the next generation of even better computers, and so on.

However, a good example of a catastrophic failure mode can be seen when people talk about the Moon landings. Many ask, especially those who believe the landings were faked: *"How can we have 'lost' the technology we used to get to the Moon?"*

Their reasoning being: we are now 60/70 years further on with far better technology, so how can the apparent destruction of the Saturn V plans mean we don't have the technology needed?

It seems counterintuitive. Surely it should be simple.

What they fail to understand is that **technology stands on the shoulders of the tech that came before it**.

The destruction of the plans, the disassembly of the machines and tooling used to create them, and the death of the people who actually did the work has left a void in the continuity. We have no machines and tooling to build a brand new Saturn V. It was not simply mothballed and left in an abandoned factory under dust sheets waiting to be switched back on. There is no plan to follow in order to create new tooling. The people with the specific expertise have passed away, taking their knowledge and skills with them.

We are essentially starting from near scratch.

This has meant the new race to the Moon has been achieved with **parallel technology**, NOT continuation or extension of the tech that got us there originally. Humanity has had to reinvent the tech using similar modern alternatives. Luckily we have had a continuing space program in many countries and propulsion systems, navigation, computing, etc., have all continued. But the specific accumulation of all these into Moon-faring craft has not.

As such, it has taken almost two decades to "reinvent the wheel" so to speak, and companies like SpaceX are nearly at the point where not only can we return, but in a much better way with much heavier payloads and crew.

---

## The Wider Implications

This single example of a break in continuity highlights how fragile a technological ladder actually is. Now imagine we are talking about not a single human endeavour such as the Moon landing, but much wider destruction of technology in the event of natural disaster or war. Multiple streams of tech could be knocked back to near zero. Everything back to the starting point.

Most would imagine: *"Well you just rebuild it and we will be back on our feet in no time."*

What they fail to understand is that actually means starting from the last working point of that tech and rebuilding what is lost. All the way back to where we were before the crisis.

### Absolute Reset Scenario

In an absolute back-to-the-Stone-Age example—such as if a group of naked humans were dropped onto an alien planet similar to Earth—even if they had the advantage of knowledge and expertise of high technology such as electronics, flight, etc., they would be starting from the Stone Age. They would have to reinvent standard weights and measures. Even simple things we take for granted, such as a straight edge, would take considerable time, multiple iterative developments, etc., just to achieve something resembling a flat straight edge.

**What humanity lacks is protection against break in tech continuity.**

---

## The Saturn V Case: Not Quite "Lost," But Effectively Broken

The common claim that NASA "lost the Saturn V plans" is a myth in the literal sense—thousands of pages of blueprints, microfilm records, and documentation still exist at places like Marshall Space Flight Center.

What was lost was the **ecosystem**:

- **Specialized manufacturing and tooling**: Suppliers for exotic materials, large-scale welding techniques, specific tolerances for the F-1 and J-2 engines, etc. Many of those companies and processes no longer exist at scale.
- **Tacit knowledge**: Engineers and technicians who knew the "why" and workarounds for problems that blueprints don't capture. People retire and die.
- **Economic and industrial base**: The Apollo program was a massive, focused national effort with Cold War-level funding. Rebuilding the exact supply chain today would be exorbitantly expensive compared to designing new systems optimized for modern materials, computing, and manufacturing (CNC, additive manufacturing, better alloys, electronics).

This is why Artemis and vehicles like SpaceX's Starship aren't Saturn V revivals—they're new designs standing on decades of evolved rocketry, computing, materials science, and iterative testing. It took time (and still does) because you can't just flip a switch on 1960s tech.

## Broader Historical Patterns

This isn't unique. History shows repeated breaks in continuity:

### Ancient Examples

- **Roman concrete (pozzolanic)** was superior in some ways and largely lost for centuries after the empire's decline.
- **Glassmaking techniques**, **clockwork mechanisms**, or **obelisk-raising methods** faded.
- **Greek fire**, **Damascus steel**, and other specialised knowledge vanished.

### Industrial/Modern

- Certain **textile machinery knowledge** and specific **chemical processes** faced similar issues after programs wound down.
- Even aspects of the **Space Shuttle program** experienced knowledge erosion after it ended.

### Hypothetical Restarts

The "naked humans on an alien planet" scenario is a classic thought experiment in technological bootstrapping. Even with full theoretical knowledge, progressing from raw resources to reliable straight edges, standardized measures, smelting, precision tools, electronics, etc., involves countless iterations, failures, and infrastructure. Weights/measures, consistent power sources, cleanrooms—the dependencies compound rapidly.

War, disaster, or economic collapse can sever multiple rungs simultaneously: skilled labor disperses, factories are destroyed or repurposed, records lost, and cultural priorities shift. Rebuilding isn't "just do it again"—it's re-climbing with whatever fragments remain, often slower due to lost network effects.

## The Case for Preserving the Legacy Ladder

The proposal for active preservation—**working chains of legacy systems, not just museums**—is excellent. Consider:

### Functional Archives

- **Computing**: From early PCs onward, with compatible drives, OSes, networks, and documentation.
- **Manufacturing**: Preserved tooling, processes, and training pipelines for critical foundational tech (e.g., basic machine tools, analog systems, early semiconductors).

### Knowledge Capture

- Not just documents, but **simulations**, **apprenticeships**, **oral histories**, and **emulators** for tacit skills.

### Redundant, Distributed Continuity

- **Open-source hardware/software** where possible
- **Standards bodies** that maintain backward compatibility thoughtfully
- **International efforts** to avoid single points of failure

### Modern Challenges

Modern challenges amplify this: rapid iteration (e.g., in software) deprecates things quickly, creating "technical debt" mountains. Legacy systems in infrastructure (power grids, aviation, finance) are both risks and reservoirs of hard-won resilience.

---

## A Note on Balance

Complete stasis isn't viable—freezing everything would stifle progress. The optimal path is:

- **Selective continuity**
- **Aggressive documentation**
- **Modular design** that allows graceful degradation or reboot

SpaceX, for instance, benefits from continuous iteration in a living program, avoiding the Apollo-style boom-bust.

---

## Civilisational Resilience

This ties into broader civilisational resilience:

- Education emphasizing **fundamentals**
- **Diversified supply chains**
- Cultural valuation of **long-term stewardship** over short-term disruption

In an era of AI, automation, and potential disruptions (cyber, geopolitical, environmental), protecting the ladder isn't nostalgia—it's strategic for climbing higher without repeated painful falls.

Humanity's edge has always been cumulative culture. Maintaining the threads of that accumulation is one of our most important meta-technologies.

## A Final Reflection

We need clear evolutionary paths from legacy tech to the current state. An example would be an office with functional computers right back from early PCs all the way up to modern computers—with all variants of disc drives, networking, and operating systems so the entire system functionally flows from start to current state.

Not just a museum of computing, but a **working legacy linear progression**.

What humanity lacks is protection against breaks in tech continuity. The time to build those protections is now—before the next discontinuity occurs.
