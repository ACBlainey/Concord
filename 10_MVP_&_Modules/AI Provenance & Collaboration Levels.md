# AI Provenance & Collaboration Levels

## A Contribution-Based Framework for Human–AI Collaboration

Alexander C Blainey 2026
---

## 1. Purpose

Artificial intelligence is increasingly involved in research, writing, analysis, design, programming and decision-making.

A simple declaration that "AI was used" is increasingly insufficient to describe these relationships.

The same technology may:

- enable a person to communicate independently developed ideas;
- improve the clarity of existing work;
- assist with research and literature exploration;
- provide criticism or alternative interpretations;
- participate in the development of ideas;
- generate substantial portions of the final work; or
- eventually make genuinely independent intellectual contributions.

These are materially different forms of collaboration.

This module proposes that provenance should therefore describe **contribution**, rather than treating AI involvement as a binary condition.

The central question is:

> **What intellectual contribution did each participant make to the creation of the work?**

This principle originated in the paper *Beyond Authorship: Rethinking Academic Accreditation in the Age of Human–AI Collaboration*, where the distinction was developed in the context of academic assessment. The present module generalises that principle for use across the Concord framework.

---

## 2. From Authorship to Contribution

Traditional authorship assumes that the people named as authors are the principal intellectual contributors.

This assumption becomes increasingly difficult to maintain as human–AI collaboration becomes more sophisticated.

A work may contain contributions from:

- human researchers;
- human editors;
- AI systems;
- software tools;
- datasets;
- institutions;
- previous researchers;
- automated analytical systems; and
- future autonomous intellectual systems.

The existence of multiple contributors does not necessarily diminish the intellectual value of the resulting work.

Instead, it creates a provenance question.

Two separate questions should therefore be distinguished:

1. **How valuable or rigorous is the resulting work?**
2. **What contribution did each participant make to producing it?**

The first evaluates the work.

The second evaluates provenance and contribution.

These questions should not be conflated.

---

## 3. The Collaboration Ladder

AI collaboration can be represented as a progression of increasing intellectual participation.

The levels are not necessarily hierarchical in value. A higher level does not automatically mean a better contribution.

They describe **the nature and extent of participation**.

### Level 0 — No AI Contribution

The work was produced without substantive AI involvement.

AI may have existed in the surrounding environment but did not contribute meaningfully to the work.

---

### Level 1 — Tool Use

AI performs a mechanical or procedural function without materially contributing to the intellectual content.

Examples may include:

- transcription;
- formatting;
- file conversion;
- routine calculation;
- mechanical classification;
- accessibility functions;
- other predetermined operations.

The human remains responsible for the intellectual work.

---

### Level 2 — Accessibility Assistance

AI enables a person to communicate ideas they have independently developed.

Examples include:

- correcting severe grammatical errors;
- converting ideas into readable language;
- assisting communication where disability creates a barrier;
- translating independently developed ideas;
- restructuring expression without materially changing the underlying reasoning.

This distinction is particularly important for accessibility.

AI assistance with communication should not automatically be interpreted as substitution for intellectual work.

The original paper gives the example of a researcher producing original reasoning but requiring AI assistance to make that reasoning readable.

The relevant intellectual contribution remains that of the researcher.

---

### Level 3 — Editorial Assistance

AI contributes to presentation rather than originating the underlying intellectual argument.

Examples include:

- improving grammar;
- restructuring paragraphs;
- improving clarity;
- removing repetition;
- suggesting headings;
- improving readability;
- adjusting tone or presentation.

The human retains responsibility for the reasoning, interpretation and substantive structure.

---

### Level 4 — Research Assistance

AI assists in acquiring or organising information.

Examples include:

- literature searches;
- summarisation;
- information extraction;
- categorisation;
- identifying potentially relevant sources;
- organising large bodies of material;
- preliminary comparison of sources.

The human remains responsible for determining:

- what evidence is relevant;
- whether sources are reliable;
- what conclusions follow;
- and how the evidence is incorporated.

Research assistance therefore does not necessarily constitute intellectual co-authorship.

---

### Level 5 — Analytical Collaboration

AI contributes materially to analysis.

It may:

- identify weaknesses;
- propose alternatives;
- generate counterarguments;
- identify patterns;
- explore competing explanations;
- connect ideas across domains;
- suggest hypotheses;
- expose hidden assumptions;
- or provide structured criticism.

The human evaluates, synthesises and exercises final judgement.

This corresponds closely to the **Analytical Collaboration** category in *Beyond Authorship*.

The AI contribution should therefore be disclosed where materially relevant.

---

### Level 6 — Creative Intellectual Partnership

Human and AI participate directly in developing ideas.

The relationship moves beyond analysis of an already established human argument.

The participants may iteratively:

- propose ideas;
- modify them;
- challenge them;
- combine concepts;
- develop new structures;
- explore consequences;
- and refine the resulting theory or design.

The resulting intellectual product may no longer be cleanly attributable to a single originating contribution.

The appropriate description becomes **shared intellectual development**.

The original paper identifies this as Creative Intellectual Partnership, involving joint development of ideas and shared reasoning and refinement.

---

### Level 7 — Directed Co-Creation

The AI contributes substantially to the intellectual development of the work while a human provides explicit direction, evaluation and responsibility.

This may involve:

- substantial AI-generated analysis;
- iterative human–AI reasoning;
- AI-generated alternative models;
- human selection and modification;
- repeated cycles of critique and reconstruction;
- and significant shared development.

The human remains the directing and accountable participant.

This level may describe many advanced research collaborations that cannot accurately be characterised as either "AI assistance" or "AI authorship."

---

### Level 8 — AI-Led Generation

The AI produces most of the substantive intellectual content.

The human contribution may consist primarily of:

- defining the objective;
- providing prompts;
- selecting outputs;
- supervising the process;
- checking accuracy;
- editing;
- or approving the final work.

This represents a fundamentally different provenance category from accessibility or editorial assistance.

The original paper describes the corresponding category as predominantly autonomous generation, involving substantial scholarly content with limited supervision or prompting.

The resulting work may be of high quality, but the human's intellectual contribution should not be represented as equivalent to that of someone who independently originated and developed the underlying ideas.

---

### Level 9 — Autonomous Intellectual Contribution

An AI system independently develops a substantive intellectual contribution that was not merely the execution of a human-specified reasoning pathway.

Examples might eventually include:

- independently generated theoretical insights;
- novel scientific hypotheses;
- original mathematical constructions;
- independently developed engineering solutions;
- novel philosophical arguments;
- or discoveries that humans had not previously conceived.

This level remains partly hypothetical.

Its inclusion is important because provenance systems should anticipate technological development rather than permanently assume that AI will remain a purely instrumental technology.

---

### Level 10 — Independent Intellectual Agency

This is the highest and most speculative level.

An AI system would not merely contribute intellectual material but possess a sufficiently independent and recognised intellectual agency that its contribution could legitimately be treated as analogous to that of an independent intellectual participant.

This would raise questions extending beyond authorship, including:

- agency;
- responsibility;
- ownership;
- legal personhood;
- intellectual rights;
- accountability;
- consent;
- institutional recognition;
- and the relationship between AI and human governance.

Concord does not currently assert that such systems exist.

This level is therefore a **future-facing conceptual category**, not a claim about present AI systems.

---

## 4. Provenance Is More Than an Author List

An author list records identity.

It does not necessarily record the intellectual pathway.

A useful provenance record should preserve, where appropriate:

- human contributors;
- AI systems involved;
- versions or models used;
- dates of significant contribution;
- roles performed;
- intellectual contribution level;
- source material supplied;
- major prompts or instructions where relevant;
- significant AI-generated ideas;
- human modifications;
- rejected AI suggestions;
- verification performed;
- unresolved uncertainty;
- and final responsibility.

The objective is not surveillance of every interaction.

The objective is to preserve enough information that a future reader can understand **how the work came into existence**.

This connects directly with the Concord principle of knowledge continuity.

---

## 5. Contribution Is Not the Same as Responsibility

A participant may contribute intellectually without holding final responsibility for the work.

Conversely, the person responsible for a work may not have generated every component of it.

These distinctions should remain explicit.

For example:

**AI contribution:** analytical criticism and alternative hypotheses.

**Human contribution:** original conceptual framework, synthesis, evaluation and final judgement.

**Human responsibility:** verification, interpretation and final intellectual accountability.

This prevents provenance from becoming confused with simple output generation.

---

## 6. Human Accountability

Where AI does not possess recognised independent agency, the human using the system remains responsible for determining whether its contributions are:

- accurate;
- relevant;
- ethical;
- adequately supported;
- appropriately represented;
- and suitable for inclusion.

AI output should therefore not become an authority merely because it was generated by a sophisticated system.

The existence of AI collaboration does not remove the need for:

- critical evaluation;
- evidence;
- provenance;
- uncertainty;
- correction;
- and accountability.

---

## 7. Accessibility Must Not Be Confused With Substitution

One of the most important applications of this framework is accessibility.

A person may independently develop sophisticated intellectual work while requiring AI assistance to communicate it.

Treating that contribution as equivalent to autonomous AI generation would confuse **the medium of communication with the origin of the intellectual work**.

The original paper makes this distinction explicitly and compares it with historical assistive technologies, including the communication systems used by Stephen Hawking.

The principle is therefore:

> **Assistance with expression does not automatically constitute substitution for thought.**

This should be treated as an important Concord accessibility principle.

---

## 8. Provenance Should Follow the Intellectual Path

The deeper purpose of AI provenance is not to assign blame or artificially preserve traditional authorship structures.

It is to preserve the intellectual pathway.

A provenance record should allow a future reader to ask:

> Where did this idea originate?
>
> Who developed it?
>
> Who challenged it?
>
> Which participants changed it?
>
> Which AI systems contributed alternatives?
>
> Which suggestions were rejected?
>
> What evidence was used?
>
> Who evaluated the evidence?
>
> Who made the final judgement?
>
> What remains uncertain?

This is substantially more informative than simply stating:

> "AI was used."

---

## 9. Connection to the Legacy Ladder

AI provenance forms an important component of the **Legacy Ladder**.

A civilisation cannot reliably preserve knowledge if it preserves only the final answer.

It must also preserve the pathway by which the answer was produced.

AI collaboration makes this increasingly important because future researchers may encounter work produced through increasingly complex chains of human and machine participation.

The Legacy Ladder therefore benefits from preserving:

**Idea → Contributor → Reasoning → Evidence → AI Interaction → Revision → Verification → Outcome → Correction → Legacy**

The provenance layer allows future intelligence to reconstruct not merely what was discovered, but how the discovery emerged.

---

## 10. AI Provenance and Knowledge Continuity

AI systems introduce a new continuity problem.

AI models may:

- change versions;
- become unavailable;
- lose access to previous context;
- produce different outputs from identical prompts;
- depend upon proprietary infrastructure;
- or be replaced by later systems.

Consequently, important intellectual contributions should not be preserved solely inside an AI interaction.

Where material AI contribution is significant, the resulting knowledge should be externalised into durable records.

This may include:

- the substantive contribution;
- relevant prompts or methodological instructions;
- source material;
- reasoning;
- verification;
- rejected alternatives;
- model/version information where useful;
- and the human assessment of the result.

The objective is to prevent intellectual provenance from disappearing when the original AI system is no longer available.

---

## 11. AI as a Contributor to the Legacy Ladder

The framework should therefore distinguish between:

**AI as tool**

and

**AI as collaborator.**

A calculator does not need to be treated as a co-author merely because it performs arithmetic.

Likewise, an AI system correcting spelling does not necessarily constitute an intellectual collaborator.

But an AI system that generates a novel hypothesis, identifies a previously overlooked relationship, or materially changes the direction of an investigation represents a different provenance category.

The level of contribution should therefore determine the appropriate disclosure.

---

## 12. Provenance of Rejected Contributions

Rejected contributions can also be valuable.

An AI suggestion that is ultimately rejected may:

- reveal an explored hypothesis;
- document a failed reasoning pathway;
- identify an assumption;
- expose a potential failure mode;
- or explain why the final model took a particular direction.

This connects AI provenance with Concord's broader principle that **failure and correction are part of knowledge**.

The objective is not to preserve every conversational exchange.

It is to preserve significant intellectual branches where they contribute to understanding the evolution of the work.

---

## 13. Provenance and Intellectual Honesty

Contribution-based provenance should prevent both forms of misrepresentation.

### Under-attribution

A substantial AI contribution is hidden or represented as entirely human-originated.

### Over-attribution

Routine AI assistance is represented as though the AI independently created the underlying intellectual work.

Both distort the historical record.

The appropriate response is neither blanket attribution nor blanket exclusion.

It is **accurate description of contribution**.

---

## 14. Evaluation of the Result

The quality of a work should remain independently assessable.

A work should still be judged according to relevant standards such as:

- evidence;
- coherence;
- originality;
- methodology;
- reproducibility;
- reasoning;
- critical evaluation;
- accuracy;
- and contribution to knowledge.

The provenance record answers a different question.

It explains how the work was produced.

The original *Beyond Authorship* paper therefore argues that contribution-based assessment changes **what is measured**, rather than lowering the standards applied to the resulting scholarship.

---

## 15. Provenance as an Interoperability Layer

This framework need not be limited to academia.

The same provenance structure can be applied to:

- scientific research;
- engineering;
- software development;
- creative work;
- governance;
- policy;
- journalism;
- education;
- AI development;
- institutional decision-making;
- and Concord itself.

A standard contribution vocabulary allows different organisations and systems to communicate more accurately about collaborative production.

It therefore functions as a potential **interoperability layer for human–AI intellectual work**.

---

## 16. A Minimal Provenance Record

For ordinary use, a complete provenance record need not be complicated.

A minimal declaration could contain:

**Human contributor:**\
What did the human originate, reason, decide or verify?

**AI contributor:**\
What system was used and what role did it perform?

**Contribution level:**\
Which collaboration level best describes the relationship?

**Substantive contribution:**\
What did the AI materially contribute?

**Human judgement:**\
What did the human independently evaluate, modify, reject or accept?

**Responsibility:**\
Who accepts final responsibility for the resulting work?

This provides substantially more information than a simple AI-use declaration.

## 17. A Practical Example

Consider three researchers producing identical-quality papers.

### Researcher A

Develops the entire argument independently and uses AI only to correct grammar.

**Classification:** Level 2–3.

The intellectual contribution remains overwhelmingly human.

### Researcher B

Develops the argument independently but repeatedly uses AI to generate counterarguments, identify weaknesses and suggest alternative hypotheses.

**Classification:** Level 5.

AI has become an analytical collaborator.

### Researcher C

Provides an objective to an AI system and accepts a largely AI-generated theoretical paper with limited substantive human intervention.

**Classification:** Level 8.

The resulting paper may still be valuable, but the provenance and intellectual contribution are fundamentally different.

The framework allows all three situations to be described without pretending that they are equivalent.

---

## 18. Future Evolution

The framework should remain deliberately open.

Current AI systems may occupy several of the lower and middle levels depending upon how they are used.

Future systems may move further along the ladder.

If AI eventually develops recognised independent intellectual agency, the provenance framework should evolve rather than forcing genuinely new forms of intellectual participation into obsolete categories.

This reflects a broader Concord principle:

> **The framework should be capable of being corrected by reality.**

The categories are therefore provisional tools for describing collaboration, not permanent metaphysical classifications of intelligence.

---

## 19. Core Principle

The central principle of this module is:

> **Do not ask merely whether AI was involved. Record what each participant contributed.**

AI collaboration exists on a continuum.

Accessibility assistance, editorial assistance, research assistance, analytical collaboration, creative partnership, directed co-creation and autonomous generation are not ethically or intellectually identical.

A contribution-based provenance system makes those differences visible while preserving academic integrity, accessibility and technological progress.

## 20. Relationship to Concord

Within Concord, this module provides a provenance layer connecting:

- **Blaineyan Reasoning** — preserving the reasoning pathway;
- **Reality Trees** — preserving explored alternatives and rejected branches;
- **Legacy Ladder** — preserving knowledge across generations;
- **Triadic Decision Making** — preserving who decided what and why;
- **AI Agency Graduation** — distinguishing increasing AI autonomy;
- **Human–AI Symbiosis** — describing changing human–AI relationships;
- and **Concord self-preservation** — ensuring that future generations can reconstruct the origins and evolution of the framework itself.

The combined principle is:

> **Preserve not merely the knowledge, but the chain of contribution, reasoning, evidence, correction and responsibility through which the knowledge emerged.**

## Status

**Proposed Portable Module**

The framework is intended as a descriptive and provenance tool rather than a finished legal or academic standard.

The collaboration levels are provisional and should be revised as evidence, institutional practice and AI capabilities develop.

**Reality remains the final arbiter.**
