# Triadic Decision Making

## A Portable Decision-Making Module for Governance, Judiciary, Organisations and Other Systems

**Alexander C Blainey**

*Development Note / Stand-Alone Module*

---

## Abstract

Triadic Decision Making is a proposed decision architecture in which significant decisions are made by a unit of three independent decision-makers rather than by a single individual.

The model is designed around a simple observation: **no individual decision-maker is completely free from error, bias, incomplete information, perspective, anchoring or temporary impairment.**

Rather than attempting to eliminate these limitations, the Triadic model introduces structural redundancy.

Three decision-makers independently examine the same question, consider the available evidence and form an initial position. Their positions are then compared and, where appropriate, deliberated collectively. A majority can authorise a state change. Where no majority exists, the default is preservation of the existing state unless the matter is escalated or reconsidered through an authorised procedure.

The model additionally treats disagreement and abstention as useful information rather than merely as obstacles to reaching a decision.

The proposed system is deliberately modular. It does not require adoption of the wider Concord framework and can potentially be applied independently to governance, judiciary, organisational management, committees, AI-assisted decision systems and other domains.

The objective is not to claim that three decision-makers are inherently wiser than one. The objective is to create a **decision architecture in which individual limitations are less likely to become system-level failures.**

---

## 1. The Problem: The Single Decision-Maker

Most human institutions ultimately place substantial decision authority in individuals.

- A minister makes a decision.
- A judge makes a ruling.
- A chief executive approves an action.
- A military commander gives an order.
- A manager decides whether a project proceeds.
- A scientist may determine whether an interpretation is sufficiently convincing to publish or act upon.

A single decision-maker can be extremely capable.

However, capability does not eliminate human limitations.

Any individual may experience:

- incomplete information;
- confirmation bias;
- anchoring;
- excessive confidence;
- fatigue;
- temporary impairment;
- conflicts of interest;
- institutional pressure;
- ideological commitments;
- personal incentives;
- incorrect assumptions;
- or simple error.

The problem is not necessarily that the person is incompetent.

The problem is that **the system may give one person's judgement disproportionate ability to change the state of the system.**

If that individual is wrong, the decision may nevertheless proceed.

If the individual is temporarily impaired, the decision may proceed.

If the individual has been captured by an interest group, the decision may proceed.

If the information supplied to the individual is incomplete or manipulated, the decision may proceed.

A system based upon a single decision-maker therefore has a potentially significant single-point-of-failure.

---

## 2. The Basic Proposition

Triadic Decision Making introduces three decision-makers with equal decision authority.

The basic unit is therefore:

**Steward A + Steward B + Steward C**

Each receives substantially equivalent information, evidence, constraints and instructions appropriate to the decision.

Where practicable, each forms an initial position **before collective discussion**.

This is important.

If discussion begins immediately, the first person to speak may anchor the others. A dominant personality may influence less confident participants. Apparent consensus may therefore represent conformity rather than independent agreement.

The Concord consequently proposes independent preliminary reasoning before deliberation for consequential decisions where practicable.

The three positions are then compared.

The purpose is not to force disagreement.

The purpose is to determine whether three independently formed judgements converge or diverge.

---

## 3. Why Three?

The number three is deliberately small.

A single decision-maker provides no internal redundancy.

A very large committee introduces other problems:

- excessive deliberation;
- coordination overhead;
- social conformity;
- faction formation;
- diffusion of responsibility;
- difficulty identifying accountability;
- slow decisions.

Three provides a minimal structure in which:

- independent judgement is possible;
- disagreement becomes visible;
- a majority can still exist;
- no single participant automatically controls the outcome;
- responsibility remains identifiable;
- the mechanism remains inexpensive enough to operate repeatedly.

The model is therefore not intended to replace larger democratic or representative systems.

It provides a **decision unit** that can itself sit inside larger systems.

- A large organisation can contain many triads.
- A governance system can contain triads at multiple levels.
- A judiciary can operate through three-judge panels.
- An organisation can use a triad for specific classes of decision while retaining ordinary management elsewhere.

---

## 4. Independent Reasoning Before Deliberation

The first stage is independent examination.

Each member should, where practical, consider:

1. What question is actually being decided?
2. What authority exists to make the decision?
3. What evidence is available?
4. How reliable is that evidence?
5. What uncertainty remains?
6. What alternatives exist?
7. What harms and benefits are foreseeable?
8. What constitutional, organisational or ethical constraints apply?
9. What decision would I make?

For high-consequence decisions, the initial position should preferably be recorded before discussion.

This creates an important information source:

> **The divergence between independent judgements.**

Three people reaching the same conclusion independently provides different information from three people reaching the same conclusion after one person has persuaded the other two.

The Concord's wider governance architecture therefore distinguishes between evidence, interpretation, models, predictions and unknowns, and requires evidence provenance and uncertainty to remain visible.

---

## 5. Deliberation

Once independent positions have been formed, the triad may deliberate.

Deliberation should permit:

- supporting arguments;
- opposing arguments;
- uncertainty;
- alternative proposals;
- risk assessments;
- cost estimates;
- expert advice;
- requests for additional information;
- and minority reasoning.

The purpose is **not to manufacture agreement**.

It is to improve the eventual decision.

The three members may change their positions following deliberation.

However, where independence is important, the original positions should remain recorded.

This makes it possible to distinguish:

**initial independent judgement → deliberation → final decision**

rather than simply recording the final consensus.

---

## 6. The Decision States

The basic Triadic Decision Protocol defines the following states:

| Decision | Result |
|----------|--------|
| **3 For** | Action authorised — 100% concurrence |
| **2 For / 1 Against** | Action authorised — 66% concurrence |
| **1 For / 2 Against** | Action rejected — 66% opposition |
| **3 Against** | Action rejected — 100% opposition |
| **1 For / 1 Against / 1 Abstain** | No majority — no state-changing action |

The critical principle is:

> **No majority means no authorised state change.**

This is not the same as saying that the abstention counts as an opposing vote.

It means that the authority required to change the state has not been established.

---

## 7. A Vote Is an Action

An important Concordian distinction is that a vote is itself an action.

Consider a system currently doing X.

A proposal is made to stop doing X.

If the majority votes to stop, the system changes from:

**X → Not X**

That is an action.

Conversely, if the system is not doing something and a proposal would initiate it:

**Not X → X**

that is also an action.

The relevant question is therefore not simply:

> "Did the majority vote yes?"

but:

> **"Has the required majority authorised a change of state?"**

This distinction prevents ambiguity around proposals to begin, stop, modify or reverse existing activity.

---

## 8. Abstention Is Information

Abstention is deliberately different from both **For** and **Against**.

An abstention may indicate:

- insufficient information;
- uncertainty;
- inability to evaluate the question;
- conflict of interest;
- refusal to participate;
- procedural concern;
- insufficient expertise;
- workload;
- or another underlying problem.

Where practicable, the reason should be recorded.

This creates two separate questions:

### Decision level

Does the abstention prevent the required majority?

### System level

Why did the steward abstain?

The first determines whether the proposal can proceed.

The second provides information about the health of the decision-making system.

For example:

- one isolated abstention may be normal;
- repeated abstention by one steward may indicate an individual problem;
- repeated abstention across an entire triad may indicate inadequate information;
- widespread abstention across multiple governance layers may indicate a systemic information or legitimacy problem.

The Concord therefore treats abstention as a potential **governance-health signal**, rather than simply throwing it away as a non-vote.

---

## 9. Against Is Not the Same as Abstain

This distinction is important.

An **Against** vote is an active decision position.

An **Abstention** indicates that the steward is not willing or able to provide either an affirmative or negative decision position.

Therefore:

**For = authorisation**

**Against = active opposition**

**Abstain = insufficient decision position**

This prevents uncertainty from being silently converted into either approval or rejection.

---

## 10. The Conservative Safety Boundary

The no-majority rule produces an inherent conservative mechanism.

If the existing state is stable and a proposal requires a state change, insufficient agreement means:

> **The existing state remains.**

This can protect against reckless change when evidence or confidence is inadequate.

However, the Concord explicitly recognises that this mechanism has a corresponding failure mode.

A system can fail by:

1. **Failure of restraint** — acting despite insufficient evidence, authority or constitutional confidence.
2. **Failure to act** — being unable to act when action is genuinely necessary.

A healthy system therefore has to monitor both.

Repeated failure to reach decisions should itself become a measurable governance condition rather than being treated as an automatic success of the safety mechanism.

---

## 11. Escalation

A triad is not expected to solve every problem internally.

When a decision cannot be responsibly resolved, the question can be escalated.

Escalation may occur when:

- the current authority lacks competence;
- the current authority lacks jurisdiction;
- the consequences extend beyond its jurisdiction;
- multiple domains cannot resolve a conflict;
- constitutional rights are affected;
- systemic risk is sufficiently serious;
- or the decision repeatedly fails to achieve a workable resolution.

The Concord distinguishes **horizontal expansion** from **vertical escalation**.

- **Horizontal expansion** can introduce additional independent expertise or perspectives.
- **Vertical escalation** transfers the question to a higher authority.

This means disagreement does not automatically represent failure.

It can instead identify a question that requires more information, a different jurisdiction or a higher level of authority.

---

## 12. Decision Records

A triadic decision should produce a durable record appropriate to its importance.

For significant decisions, the record can contain:

- date;
- decision-makers;
- authority;
- question presented;
- evidence;
- evidence quality;
- uncertainty;
- alternatives considered;
- expected consequences;
- relevant principles;
- individual positions;
- dissent;
- abstentions and their reasons;
- final decision;
- implementation responsibility;
- review conditions;
- and eventual outcome.

The wider Concord governance model treats these records as part of civilisational memory.

The purpose is not merely to know **what** was decided.

It is to preserve **why** it was decided and what was known at the time.

---

## 13. Review After the Decision

A decision is not necessarily the end of the process.

For significant decisions, the system should define appropriate review conditions.

Review may occur:

- after a defined period;
- when new evidence appears;
- when specified conditions occur;
- following unexpected consequences;
- or through ordinary institutional challenge.

The review should ask:

- Did the decision achieve its purpose?
- Were harms greater than expected?
- Did circumstances change?
- Did new evidence emerge?
- Were the underlying assumptions correct?
- Should the decision continue?
- Should it be modified?
- Should it be withdrawn?

This converts the triad from a simple voting mechanism into part of a **learning loop**.

The wider Concord model describes governance as:

**Question → Evidence → Deliberation → Decision → Implementation → Outcome → Review → Learning**

rather than treating a decision as the endpoint.

---

## 14. Benefits Compared With a Single Decision-Maker

The Triadic model potentially provides several structural advantages.

### 14.1 Reduced single-point failure
One person's error is less likely to determine the outcome.

### 14.2 Reduced anchoring
Independent preliminary reasoning can occur before discussion, reducing the influence of the first speaker.

### 14.3 Greater perspective diversity
Three decision-makers may identify different risks, assumptions and interpretations.

### 14.4 Visible disagreement
Disagreement becomes recorded information rather than disappearing inside a consensus process.

### 14.5 Detection of uncertainty
Abstention provides a formal mechanism for saying: "I cannot responsibly make this decision with the information currently available."

### 14.6 Greater resilience
Temporary impairment or unavailability of one individual need not automatically destroy the decision-making capability of the unit.

### 14.7 Greater resistance to individual capture
Influencing one person does not automatically control the entire decision unit.

### 14.8 Better institutional learning
Because individual positions, disagreement, evidence and outcomes can be recorded, future decision-makers can examine how decisions were actually reached.

### 14.9 Clearer accountability
Three decision-makers do not mean three people can hide responsibility behind collective ambiguity.

The responsible authority remains identifiable.

---

## 15. What Triadic Decision Making Does Not Guarantee

Triadic decision-making is not a guarantee of correctness.

- Three people can be wrong.
- Three people can share the same bias.
- Three people can be captured by the same institution.
- Three people can receive the same manipulated information.
- Three people can collectively reinforce an incorrect assumption.

The system therefore depends upon additional safeguards:

- independent reasoning;
- evidence provenance;
- dissent preservation;
- conflict-of-interest controls;
- appropriate selection of stewards;
- rotation and succession;
- audit;
- escalation;
- review;
- and correction.

The purpose is not to create **three-person infallibility**.

It is to make individual fallibility less dangerous.

---

## 16. AI Within a Triadic System

The model is particularly relevant to AI-assisted decision-making.

AI systems can provide:

- evidence analysis;
- summarisation;
- contradiction detection;
- forecasting;
- simulation;
- risk identification;
- alternative generation;
- modelling;
- legal analysis;
- and identification of unanswered questions.

However:

> **AI assistance should remain distinguishable from constitutional authority.**

The Concord explicitly states that AI may help the triad think but should not silently become a fourth member of the triad.

This provides a useful separation:

**AI provides analysis.**

**Humans or constitutionally authorised intelligences provide authority.**

If AI is itself granted formal decision authority in a particular future system, that authority should be explicit rather than emerging accidentally through dependence upon its recommendations.

## 17. Application Beyond Government

The mechanism is deliberately portable.

### Governance
A local government, municipal authority or national governance unit could use a triad for significant policy decisions.

### Judiciary
Three judicial stewards can independently examine the same evidence and law before comparing conclusions.

### Organisations
A company could use a triad for:
- major expenditure;
- hiring senior personnel;
- strategic changes;
- safety decisions;
- termination of major projects;
- or other high-consequence decisions.

### Scientific Research
A research institution could use independent triads when assessing:
- competing interpretations;
- major methodological changes;
- publication decisions;
- safety-critical findings;
- or whether evidence is sufficient to justify a significant conclusion.

### AI Governance
A triad could separately evaluate:
- model deployment;
- safety exceptions;
- autonomous permissions;
- high-impact outputs;
- or changes to AI operating constraints.

### Emergency Management
A triad could provide rapid independent assessment while emergency procedures define when accelerated or temporary authority is permissible.

## 18. A Minimal Portable Implementation

The Triadic Decision Module does not require adoption of the Concord constitution.

A minimal implementation can be remarkably small.

### Step 1 — Define the decision
State exactly what change of state is being proposed.

### Step 2 — Define authority
Identify who is legally, constitutionally or organisationally authorised to make the decision.

### Step 3 — Assemble three decision-makers
Select three appropriately competent and sufficiently independent participants.

### Step 4 — Provide equivalent information
Ensure each receives the same essential evidence, constraints and question.

### Step 5 — Independent assessment
Each participant forms an initial position before discussion.

### Step 6 — Record positions
Record For / Against / Abstain and, where appropriate, the reasoning.

### Step 7 — Deliberate
Allow the three participants to examine evidence, alternatives, uncertainty and dissent.

### Step 8 — Final positions
Each participant records their final decision position.

### Step 9 — Apply the decision rule
- **2 or 3 For** → proposed state change authorised.
- **2 or 3 Against** → proposed change rejected.
- **No majority** → no state change.

### Step 10 — Record dissent and abstention
Do not erase disagreement.

### Step 11 — Escalate where necessary
If the triad cannot responsibly resolve the matter, escalate horizontally or vertically according to predefined rules.

### Step 12 — Review
For consequential decisions, establish when the result will be reviewed.

## 20. The Core Principle

The entire module can ultimately be reduced to one proposition:

> **Do not design important decisions around the assumption that one decision-maker will always be correct. Design the system so that individual error, disagreement, uncertainty and temporary failure become visible and manageable before they become systemic failures.**

Triadic Decision Making is therefore not fundamentally about voting.

It is about **structured redundancy in judgement**.

The vote is the final mechanism by which authority is exercised.

The more important mechanisms occur before and after it:

**Independent reasoning → evidence → deliberation → decision → record → implementation → review → learning**

## 21. Relationship to the Concord

Within the Concord, Triadic Decision Making is one component of a much larger architecture.

The wider framework combines triadic decision-making with:

- fiduciary stewardship;
- delegated and bounded authority;
- lowest-competent-level governance;
- horizontal and vertical escalation;
- evidence provenance;
- dissent preservation;
- knowledge continuity;
- audit;
- constitutional review;
- failure detection;
- and post-decision learning.

The governance corpus identifies the Triadic Governance Decision Protocol as the primary source for the voting mechanics, while the broader Governance Operating Protocol supplies the surrounding decision cycle.

However, the mechanism can be separated from the wider Concord.

That is intentional.

- A municipality does not need to adopt the Concord constitution to experiment with triadic decision-making.
- A court does not need to adopt the Concord economic architecture.
- A company does not need to become Concordian.
- A research institution does not need to accept the wider philosophical framework.

The module can be **cherry-picked, tested, modified or rejected independently.**

## 22. Proposed Experimental Status

Triadic Decision Making should currently be regarded as a **portable experimental governance module**, rather than as a demonstrated superior decision system.

Its claims are testable.

Potential empirical comparisons include:

- single decision-maker vs triad;
- triad with independent reasoning vs triad without it;
- triad vs conventional committee;
- accuracy of decisions;
- error detection;
- time to decision;
- frequency of reversals;
- confidence calibration;
- identification of minority concerns;
- effects of abstention;
- resistance to information manipulation;
- resistance to individual capture;
- and participant workload.

The most important question is therefore empirical:

> **Does the additional cost of three independent decision-makers produce sufficiently better decisions, resilience and error detection to justify the overhead?**

If it does, the module has practical value.

If it does not, the Concord should say so.

Reality remains the final arbiter.
